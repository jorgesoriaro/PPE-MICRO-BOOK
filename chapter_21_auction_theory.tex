\chapter{Auction Theory}

\section{Learning objectives}

After completing this chapter, you should be able to:
\begin{itemize}
  \item explain why an auction can reveal information that a posted price cannot;
  \item distinguish private, common, and interdependent values;
  \item compare English, Dutch, first-price, and second-price auction formats;
  \item derive and interpret a simple first-price bidding benchmark;
  \item explain truthful bidding in a private-value second-price auction; and
  \item diagnose the winner's curse and evaluate basic auction-design choices.
\end{itemize}

\section{Why use an auction?}

An \keyterm{auction} is a rule for allocating an object through competing
offers. It is useful when a seller does not know in advance how much different
buyers value an item, or when the item has no obvious market price. A
government deciding who may operate a scarce radio frequency, a university
selling a charity item, and a firm choosing a supplier all face versions of
this problem.

At a posted price, the seller selects the price before learning much about
buyers' willingness to pay. An auction instead asks participants to reveal
some information through bids or decisions to remain active. This does not
make auctions automatically fair or efficient. It simply changes the
information and incentives that shape allocation.

For bidder \(i\), let \(v_i\) denote the value of receiving the object and
let \(p_i\) denote the payment. If the bidder wins, the monetary surplus is
\[
v_i-p_i.
\]
The central strategic question is therefore not only whether to win, but at
what price and under which rule.

\begin{keyidea}
\textbf{An auction is a mechanism.}
It specifies who may participate, what they can bid, who receives the object,
what the winner pays, and how rules are enforced. As in Chapter~18, the design
of those rules changes the incentives of strategic participants.
\end{keyidea}

\section{Values and information}

The right bidding strategy depends on what a bidder knows about value. In a
\keyterm{private-value auction}, each participant's value is known to that
participant and is independent of others' information. A concert ticket for
personal use is often close to this case: another person's enthusiasm need not
change how much the ticket is worth to you.

In a \keyterm{common-value auction}, the object has roughly the same
underlying value for everyone, but that value is uncertain. Bidders observe
different clues. Rights to exploit a natural resource, an antique with an
uncertain resale value, or a contract with uncertain future costs can have
important common-value elements. In an \keyterm{interdependent-values}
setting, a bidder's value is private but other bidders' information can still
change it. A home buyer, for example, may value a property partly for personal
reasons and partly for an uncertain resale price.

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.2}
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{2.55cm}p{3.65cm}p{4.65cm}@{}}
  \toprule
  Setting & What bidder \(i\) knows & Why other bids matter\\
  \midrule
  Private value & Own value \(v_i\) & Mainly because they determine whether \(i\) wins and what \(i\) pays.\\
  Common value & A signal about a shared value \(V\) & They reveal whether \(i\)'s signal was unusually optimistic.\\
  Interdependent values & Own value and a signal about others' values & They can contain useful information about the object's value or quality.\\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

These are models, not rigid labels. A car may be a private-value purchase for
one buyer but have a common-value component when its reliability is uncertain.
The practical question is whether winning itself tells the winner something
unfavourable about the object.

\section{Bidding incentives: first price and second price}

In a first-price sealed-bid auction, the winner pays their own bid. A high bid
makes winning more likely, but leaves less surplus conditional on winning. If
\(b_i\) is bidder \(i\)'s bid, the expected payoff can be written as
\[
\pi_i(b_i)
 =
\bigl(v_i-b_i\bigr)
\Pr\!\left(b_i>\max_{j\ne i}b_j\right).
\]
This is the logic of \keyterm{bid shading}: a bidder normally bids below
their private value to retain some surplus if they win.

Here is a useful benchmark. Suppose there are \(n\) risk-neutral bidders with
independent private values uniformly distributed on \([0,\bar v]\). In the
symmetric equilibrium of this simple model,
\[
b(v)=\frac{n-1}{n}v.
\]
With two bidders, a value of \euro60 leads to a bid of \euro30; with three
bidders, the benchmark bid is \euro40. More competitors make bidding more
aggressive because winning becomes harder.

\paragraph{A compact derivation.}
Suppose rivals use \(b=\alpha v\), and a bidder with true value \(v\) bids as
if their value were \(z\). The probability of beating all rivals is
\((z/\bar v)^{n-1}\), so expected payoff is
\[
\pi(v,z)
=
\bigl(v-\alpha z\bigr)
\left(\frac{z}{\bar v}\right)^{n-1}.
\]
The first-order condition is
\[
(n-1)(v-\alpha z)-\alpha z=0.
\]
In a symmetric equilibrium \(z=v\), giving
\(\alpha=(n-1)/n\). This calculation is deliberately narrow: risk aversion,
asymmetric bidders, correlated values, or a different format can change the
answer.

In a second-price sealed-bid auction, a bidder's own bid changes whether they
win but not the price paid if they win. Let \(h\) be the highest rival bid. If
\(h<v_i\), winning gives positive surplus and any bid above \(h\) is desirable.
If \(h>v_i\), winning gives negative surplus and any bid below \(h\) is
desirable. Bidding exactly \(v_i\) makes the correct choice in both cases.
Thus truthful bidding is weakly dominant under the private-value assumptions
of this model. Chapter~18 showed the same logic as an incentive-compatible
mechanism.

\begin{keyidea}
\textbf{Do not confuse format with outcome.}
The standard models predict that some formats can be strategically equivalent
under strong assumptions. For example, a Dutch auction and a first-price
sealed-bid auction have the same trade-off in the independent private-value
benchmark. That does not mean they are interchangeable in practice: public
information, time pressure, participation, and rules against collusion can
matter greatly.
\end{keyidea}

\section{Common values and the winner's curse}

The \keyterm{winner's curse} is not irrationality. It is a selection problem.
When several bidders estimate the same uncertain value, the highest estimate
is likely to contain the most optimistic error. Winning is therefore new
information: it can lower a rational bidder's estimate of what the object is
really worth.

\FloatBarrier

The relevant comparison is often
\[
\text{maximum sensible bid}
\leq
\mathbb{E}[V\mid \text{win},s_i].
\]
Risk aversion, costs of participation, or a desired profit margin may push the
bid still lower. Sellers can reduce the winner's curse by allowing inspection,
disclosing credible information, or choosing a format in which rivals'
decisions reveal useful information. These choices may also increase entry,
because bidders are less afraid that winning means overpaying.

\section{Designing auctions and procurement rules}

Auction design is an applied exercise in mechanism design. A designer must
decide not only who wins, but what objective the auction serves. Revenue is
one objective; allocating the object to the person who values it most,
attracting qualified participants, limiting collusion, and maintaining public
legitimacy are others.

Procurement makes the contrast especially clear. A \keyterm{reverse auction}
asks suppliers to compete to provide a good or service, so the lowest
acceptable bid tends to win. But awarding a complex contract solely to the
lowest price can encourage unrealistic bids, poor quality, or later
renegotiation. A serious procurement rule may therefore specify quality
standards, verification, penalties, and transparent evaluation criteria as
well as price.

\begin{keyidea}
\textbf{Five design questions for an auction.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item What is the objective: efficient allocation, revenue, low procurement cost, access, or something else?
  \item What is privately known, and what information can be credibly disclosed or verified?
  \item Which bids are eligible, and is there a \keyterm{reserve price} or quality threshold?
  \item Does the payment rule encourage truthful information, entry, and serious bids?
  \item How will the rule deter collusion, enforce promises, and remain understandable to participants?
\end{enumerate}
\end{keyidea}

The answer depends on institutional context. A well-designed auction does not
remove ethical choices; it makes them explicit. Selling a public asset to the
highest bidder may raise revenue, while allocating it to the bidder who will
provide the best service may advance a different public objective.

\section*{Chapter Review}

\subsection*{Key concepts and equations}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Private-value auction}: each bidder knows their own value, which is independent of others' information.
  \item \textbf{Common-value auction}: bidders have different signals about an uncertain underlying value shared by all.
  \item \textbf{English and Dutch auctions}: public formats with ascending and descending prices, respectively.
  \item \textbf{First-price auction}: highest bidder wins and pays their own bid; bid shading balances margin against the chance of winning.
  \item \textbf{Second-price auction}: highest bidder wins and pays the second-highest bid; truthful bidding is weakly dominant under private values.
  \item \textbf{Winner's curse}: winning conveys adverse information in a common-value setting and can make a naive bid too high.
  \item \textbf{Benchmark first-price bid}: with \(n\) symmetric risk-neutral bidders and uniform private values, \(b(v)=\frac{n-1}{n}v\).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Exercises}

\subsection*{Conceptual questions}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.]
  \item Why might a seller prefer an auction to a posted price when buyers have different private valuations?
  \item Classify each case as mainly private value, common value, or interdependent values: a ticket for your favourite band; drilling rights with uncertain reserves; and a house with an uncertain resale value.
  \item Compare English, Dutch, first-price sealed-bid, and second-price sealed-bid auctions in terms of information revealed and payment rule.
  \item Why does a first-price bidder normally shade their bid below their valuation?
  \item Explain the winner's curse. Why can better disclosure encourage rather than discourage participation?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Graphical exercises}
\begin{enumerate}[resume,label=\arabic*.]

  \item Recreate the information flow in Figure~\ref{fig:winners-curse} for a bidder choosing whether to bid on a contract with uncertain costs. Explain the direction of the bid adjustment.

\subsection*{Analytical exercises}
\begin{enumerate}[resume,label=\arabic*.]
  \item Three students value a signed football shirt at \euro30, \euro22, and \euro15. Under truthful bids in a second-price sealed-bid auction, identify the winner, payment, and each student's net payoff.
  \item Under the symmetric benchmark \(b(v)=\frac{n-1}{n}v\), calculate the first-price bid for a bidder with value \euro60 when \(n=2\), and when \(n=3\). State two assumptions needed for this formula.
  \item A bidder's initial signal suggests a common value of \euro100. Conditional on winning, the bidder estimates that the expected actual value is only \euro85. In a first-price auction with no other costs, what is the highest bid that avoids an expected loss? Explain why the conditional estimate is the relevant one.
  \item In a reverse auction, suppliers \(A\), \(B\), and \(C\) have verified costs of \euro40, \euro55, and \euro70. Explain why \(A\) should not automatically bid \euro40. If \(A\) wins a fixed-price contract at \euro52, calculate its margin and name one non-price rule the buyer could use to protect quality.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Exam-style multiple-choice questions}
\begin{enumerate}[resume,label=\arabic*.]
  \item In a second-price sealed-bid auction, the winner pays: (a) their own bid; (b) the reserve price only; (c) the second-highest bid; or (d) nothing.
  \item In the standard independent private-value model, a first-price bidder shades a bid below value because: (a) the bidder never wants to win; (b) a higher bid raises the chance of winning but lowers surplus if the bidder wins; (c) bids are always public; or (d) the winner pays the second-highest bid.
  \item The winner's curse is especially relevant when: (a) several bidders have noisy signals about a common underlying value; (b) each bidder knows a private value with no uncertainty; (c) a market is perfectly competitive; or (d) a public good is non-rival.
\end{enumerate}
