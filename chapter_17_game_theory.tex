\section{Learning objectives}

After completing this chapter, you should be able to:
\begin{itemize}
  \item identify players, strategies, payoffs, and rules in a game;
  \item find dominant strategies, best responses, and Nash equilibria;
  \item analyse sequential games by backward induction; and
  \item explain credibility, subgame perfection, and repeated interaction.
\end{itemize}

\section{Strategic interaction}

\keyterm{Game theory} studies situations in which each person's outcome depends on their own action and the actions of others. A game specifies players, strategies, payoffs, and rules. Strategic interaction arises in oligopoly, bargaining, political campaigns, international relations, and everyday coordination problems.

\section{Strategic-form games}

A \keyterm{strategic-form game} represents choices in a payoff matrix. A strategy is \keyterm{dominant} if it gives a player a higher payoff than any alternative regardless of opponents' actions. A \keyterm{best response} is the strategy that maximises a player's payoff for a given opponent action.

A \keyterm{Nash equilibrium} is a profile of strategies in which every player's choice is a best response to the choices of others. No player can gain by changing their action unilaterally. Nash equilibrium predicts stability, not necessarily cooperation, fairness, or efficiency.

The Prisoner's Dilemma illustrates this distinction: individually rational self-protective choices can produce an outcome that both players regard as worse than cooperation. Institutions, contracts, social norms, and repeated interaction can matter precisely because they change incentives.

\section{Sequential games and backward induction}

In \keyterm{dynamic games}, players move in sequence and may observe earlier actions. A game tree records the order of moves, choices, and payoffs. \keyterm{Backward induction} solves a finite game by first determining what a player will do at the final decision node, then working back to earlier choices.

A threat is \keyterm{credible} only if it is in the threatening player's interest to carry it out when the relevant moment arrives. A \keyterm{subgame-perfect equilibrium} specifies a Nash equilibrium in every subgame and therefore rules out non-credible threats.

Repeated interaction can support cooperation when future consequences are important, actions are observable, and deviations can be punished. It does not guarantee cooperation: trust, enforcement, patience, and the possibility of misunderstanding remain important.

\section*{Chapter Review}

\subsection*{Key concepts}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Strategy}: complete action available to a player.
  \item \textbf{Dominant strategy}: best regardless of opponents' actions.
  \item \textbf{Nash equilibrium}: mutual best responses.
  \item \textbf{Backward induction}: reasoning from final choices backward.
  \item \textbf{Credible threat}: action optimal when the contingency occurs.
  \item \textbf{Subgame-perfect equilibrium}: equilibrium in every subgame.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Exercises}

\subsection*{Conceptual questions}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.]
  \item Define a Nash equilibrium. Why need it not be Pareto efficient?
  \item Distinguish a dominant strategy from a best response.
  \item Explain backward induction and credibility.
  \item How can repeated interaction support cooperation?
\end{enumerate}

  \item Draw a two-stage entry-deterrence game tree and mark the solution obtained by backward induction.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Analytical exercises}
\begin{enumerate}[resume,label=\arabic*.]
  \item In the matrix with $(A1,B1)=(4,4)$, $(A1,B2)=(1,5)$, $(A2,B1)=(5,1)$, and $(A2,B2)=(2,2)$, identify dominant strategies, Nash equilibrium, and Pareto-efficient outcomes.
  \item An entrant chooses Enter or Stay Out. If Enter, an incumbent chooses Fight or Accommodate. Construct payoffs where Fight is not credible and find the subgame-perfect equilibrium.
  \item Give a political or economic example in which individually rational choices create an inefficient collective result.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Exam-style multiple-choice questions}
\begin{enumerate}[resume,label=\arabic*.]
  \item A Nash equilibrium is an outcome where: (a) every player cooperates; (b) no player benefits by unilaterally changing action; (c) all payoffs are equal; or (d) one player wins.
  \item In a dynamic game, backward induction begins by analysing: (a) the first move; (b) the final decision nodes; (c) average payoffs; or (d) market demand.
  \item A subgame-perfect equilibrium excludes: (a) all cooperation; (b) credible actions; (c) non-credible threats; or (d) sequential decisions.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{PPE discussion question}

\noindent When strategic interaction produces a socially undesirable equilibrium, should policy change payoffs, restrict strategies, or encourage cooperation? Discuss with one economic or political example.
