\chapter{The Basics of Mechanism Design}
\label{chap:mechanism-design}

\section{Learning objectives}

After completing this chapter, you should be able to:
\begin{itemize}
  \item distinguish game theory from mechanism design;
  \item identify a mechanism's objective, messages, allocation rule, transfers, and information;
  \item define private types, incentive compatibility, and participation constraints;
  \item explain why a second-price auction encourages truthful bidding under private values; and
  \item compare efficiency, equity, simplicity, and feasibility in the design of a real allocation rule.
\end{itemize}

\section{From predicting games to designing rules}

Game theory starts with a set of rules and asks what strategic participants
will do. \keyterm{Mechanism design} reverses the direction. It begins with an
objective---such as allocating a scarce resource well---and asks which rules
will lead self-interested participants to reveal information or take actions
that produce a desirable outcome.

The designer may be a government allocating school places, a hospital assigning
appointments, a platform matching drivers and passengers, a firm procuring a
service, or a group choosing a collective project. In each case the challenge
is that people often know more than the designer about their own values, costs,
needs, or abilities.

\begin{keyidea}
\textbf{A mechanism is not only an algorithm.}
It is a complete rule for interaction: it tells participants what they may
report or do, how those reports affect allocation and payment, and what happens
if they decline to participate. A technically elegant rule that people cannot
understand, verify, or regard as legitimate may fail in practice.
\end{keyidea}

\section{The ingredients of a mechanism}

A \keyterm{mechanism} specifies the messages participants can send, the
\keyterm{allocation rule} that maps messages into an outcome, and any
\keyterm{transfers} such as prices, taxes, rewards, or penalties. A
\keyterm{type} is the private information relevant to a participant's choice:
for example, a valuation for a permit, the cost of supplying a service, or the
severity of a medical need.

For \(n\) participants, let \(m=(m_1,\ldots,m_n)\) denote the reported
messages. A mechanism can be represented compactly by an allocation
\(x(m)\) and transfers \(t_i(m)\) for each participant \(i\). The notation is
simple, but it forces useful questions: what can people report, what allocation
results, and who pays or receives what?

\section{Direct mechanisms and truthful reporting}

A \keyterm{direct mechanism} asks participants to report their types directly.
If student \(i\)'s value for a place is \(\theta_i\), the student reports
\(\hat{\theta}_i\). The report can be truthful,
\(\hat{\theta}_i=\theta_i\), or strategic. The mechanism then uses all reports
to choose the allocation and transfers.

    \draw[ppearrow] (allocation) -- (payment);

  \label{fig:direct-mechanism-flow}

\FloatBarrier

Suppose utility is quasi-linear: a participant values the allocated outcome and
then pays a transfer. With positive \(t_i\) denoting a payment by participant
\(i\),
\[
u_i=v_i\!\left(x_i(\hat{\theta}_i,\theta_{-i}),\theta_i\right)
    -t_i(\hat{\theta}_i,\theta_{-i}).
\]
Here \(\theta_{-i}\) denotes the other participants' types. A mechanism is
\keyterm{incentive compatible} if a truthful report is at least as good as a
misreport:
\[
u_i(\theta_i,\theta_i,\theta_{-i})
\geq
u_i(\theta_i,\hat{\theta}_i,\theta_{-i})
\qquad\text{for every feasible }\hat{\theta}_i.
\]

Incentive compatibility concerns the choice \emph{within} the mechanism. A
mechanism is \keyterm{individually rational} when joining is worthwhile
relative to a person's outside option \(\bar u_i\):
\[
u_i(\theta_i,\theta_i,\theta_{-i})\geq\bar u_i.
\]
This is also called a \keyterm{participation constraint}. A student may report
truthfully yet still decline a mechanism that makes participation too costly or
offers too little benefit.

\begin{keyidea}
\textbf{Two different tests.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Incentive compatibility:} Does it make sense to follow the intended rule, such as reporting truthfully?
  \item \textbf{Individual rationality:} Is it better to participate than to use the outside option?
\end{itemize}
\end{keyidea}

\section{A simple allocation example: a second-price auction}

Consider one scarce seminar place and two students. Student \(A\) values the
place at \euro30 and student \(B\) at \euro20. In a
\keyterm{second-price auction}, the highest bidder receives the object but
pays the second-highest bid. If both bid truthfully, \(A\) receives the place,
pays \euro20, and obtains a surplus of \euro10. Student \(B\) neither receives
the place nor pays.

\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.2}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
  \toprule
  & Student \(A\) & Student \(B\)\\
  \midrule
  Private value & \euro30 & \euro20\\
  Truthful bid & \euro30 & \euro20\\
  Allocation & Receives place & Does not receive place\\
  Payment & \euro20 & \euro0\\
  Net payoff & \euro10 & \euro0\\
  \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Why is truthful bidding a \emph{weakly dominant} strategy in this setting?
Let \(b_{-i}\) be the other bid and \(v_i\) be student \(i\)'s value. If
\(b_{-i}<v_i\), bidding above \(b_{-i}\) wins at price \(b_{-i}\), so bidding
too low can lose a profitable allocation. If \(b_{-i}>v_i\), truthful bidding
loses and gives payoff zero, while bidding above \(b_{-i}\) wins but pays more
than the item is worth. A bid of \(v_i\) avoids both mistakes. It need not be
the only best bid in every case, which is why the dominance is weak.

The result depends on assumptions: private values, a genuine second-price rule,
and no important collusion or bidding costs. Chapter~19 develops auction
formats and their limits in more detail.

\section{Mechanisms are more than markets}

Prices are only one way to allocate scarce resources. For school places,
medical appointments, social housing, or immigration slots, the objective may
give substantial weight to need, equal treatment, or rights rather than only
to willingness to pay. Table~\ref{tab:allocation-mechanisms} compares four
familiar mechanisms.

  \centering
  \small
  \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.18}
  \begin{tabular}{p{2.55cm}p{3.55cm}p{5.35cm}}
    \toprule
    Mechanism & Main allocation logic & Incentive or ethical question\\
    \midrule
    Price or auction & Highest willingness to pay & Efficient for a narrow value criterion, but ability to pay may be ethically irrelevant.\\
    Lottery & Equal chance among eligible applicants & Simple and transparent, but may ignore urgency or differences in benefit.\\
    Priority by need & Applicants with higher assessed need receive priority & Requires information and verification; applicants may exaggerate need.\\
    Queue & Earlier arrival receives priority & Easy to administer, but may waste time and favour those with flexible schedules.\\
    \bottomrule

  different strategic incentives.}
  \label{tab:allocation-mechanisms}
\end{table}

No mechanism is neutral. A designer must state which criterion matters and
then evaluate whether the rule actually advances it. A lottery may be more
legitimate than a price in one setting; a price may communicate useful
information in another. The relevant comparison is between feasible
alternatives, not between a real mechanism and an idealised one.

\section{Trade-offs, limits, and legitimacy}

Mechanism design does not produce a magic rule. Desirable properties can
conflict. \keyterm{Budget balance} requires that a mechanism not need an
outside subsidy; efficiency can require payments or information that are hard
to obtain; fairness can call for priority rules that are difficult to verify.
Complex rules may be manipulable, invasive of privacy, expensive to administer,
or too difficult for participants to understand.

The final assessment is therefore institutional as well as mathematical. Does
the mechanism work with realistic information, enforcement capacity, and
strategic sophistication? Who gains, who loses, and who has the power to
challenge its decisions? These questions connect the formal analysis back to
the PPE framework developed in Chapter~15.

\section*{Chapter Review}

\subsection*{Key concepts and equations}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Mechanism design}: design of rules that produce desired outcomes with strategic participants.
  \item \textbf{Type}: private information relevant to a participant's choices, such as value, cost, or need.
  \item \textbf{Direct mechanism}: rule in which participants report their types directly.
  \item \textbf{Allocation rule}: mapping from messages to an allocation.
  \item \textbf{Transfer}: payment, reward, tax, or penalty determined by the mechanism.
  \item \textbf{Incentive compatibility}: intended behaviour is optimal for each participant.
  \item \textbf{Individual rationality}: participation is at least as good as the outside option.
  \item \textbf{Budget balance}: the mechanism does not require a net outside subsidy.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Exercises}

\subsection*{Conceptual questions}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.]
  \item Distinguish game theory from mechanism design.
  \item Identify the objective, private information, messages, allocation rule, and transfers in a mechanism for allocating student housing.
  \item What is a private type? Give two examples from public policy or markets.
  \item Distinguish incentive compatibility from individual rationality.
  \item Why must equity or legitimacy be stated as an objective rather than assumed to follow from efficiency?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Graphical exercises}
\begin{enumerate}[resume,label=\arabic*.]
  \item Draw a direct-mechanism flow for allocating medical appointments. Identify the private type, report, allocation rule, and consequence of a false report.
  \item Create a two-column comparison of a lottery and a need-based priority rule for a scarce public service. State one advantage and one incentive problem for each.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Analytical exercises}
\begin{enumerate}[resume,label=\arabic*.]
  \item Two students value a scarce seminar place at \euro30 and \euro20. Explain the allocation, payments, and net payoffs under a truthful second-price sealed-bid auction.
  \item A student's value is \euro30. If the other bid is \euro20, compare bids of \euro15, \euro30, and \euro45. Then repeat when the other bid is \euro40. Explain why truthful bidding is weakly dominant.
  \item A low-need applicant gains \(B=\text{\euro6}\) from falsely claiming high need. The claim is audited with probability \(p=0.25\), and a false claim carries penalty \(F\). Derive the minimum \(F\) that makes truthful reporting incentive compatible.
  \item Propose a mechanism for allocating a scarce public service when monetary payments are prohibited. State its objective, the private information involved, one participation issue, and one possible strategic response.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Exam-style multiple-choice questions}
\begin{enumerate}[resume,label=\arabic*.]
  \item Mechanism design primarily asks: (a) how players behave under fixed rules; (b) what rules induce desirable behaviour; (c) how to eliminate all private information; or (d) how to avoid incentives.
  \item A mechanism is incentive compatible when: (a) all participants receive the same payoff; (b) the intended strategy is optimal for participants; (c) participation is mandatory; or (d) no transfers occur.
  \item Individual rationality requires that: (a) every participant tells the truth; (b) the allocation maximises total value; (c) participation is at least as good as the outside option; or (d) the designer earns a profit.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{PPE discussion question}

\noindent Should access to scarce public goods---such as school places, medical
appointments, or social housing---be allocated through prices, queues,
lotteries, or need-based rules? Compare the incentives and ethical implications
of at least two mechanisms, then defend one feasible design.
