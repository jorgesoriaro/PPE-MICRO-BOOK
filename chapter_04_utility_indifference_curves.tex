\chapter{Utility and Indifference Curves}
\label{chap:utility}

\section{Learning objectives}

After completing this chapter, you should be able to:
\begin{itemize}
  \item explain how utility represents preferences;
  \item distinguish ordinal from cardinal utility;
  \item draw and interpret indifference curves;
  \item calculate marginal utility and the marginal rate of substitution; and
  \item recognise the shapes of indifference curves for common preferences.
\end{itemize}

\section{Utility as a representation of preferences}

Preferences rank consumption bundles. A \keyterm{utility function} assigns a
number to each bundle in a way that represents this ranking. Think of the
number as a score for ordering options, not as a thermometer for happiness.
More precisely, $u$ represents a preference relation $\succeq$ when, for
every pair of bundles $A$ and $B$,
\[
A\succeq B
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
u(A)\geq u(B).
\]
Consequently, $A\succ B$ if and only if $u(A)>u(B)$, and $A\sim B$ if and
only if $u(A)=u(B)$.

For example, the utility function
\[
u(x,y)=xy
\]
represents a consumer who values both goods. The bundle $(2,6)$ gives utility
$12$, while $(3,5)$ gives utility $15$, so the latter is preferred. The
numbers do not measure happiness in an objective sense. Their only job here
is to preserve the ordering of bundles.

\section{Ordinal and cardinal utility}

In standard consumer theory, utility is \keyterm{ordinal}. Only the ranking
matters, not the size of utility differences. Unlike euros or kilometres, a
utility unit has no fixed common scale. If $u(x,y)=xy$ represents a
consumer's preferences, then $v(x,y)=100xy+7$ represents exactly the same
preferences: both functions rank all bundles in the same order. More
generally, if $u(x,y)$ represents preferences, then
\[
v(x,y)=g(u(x,y))
\]
represents the same ordinal preferences whenever $g$ is strictly increasing.
The transformation $v=100u+7$ is one simple example. Utility numbers can be
rescaled without changing the consumer's ranking.

This is different from \keyterm{cardinal utility}, in which numerical
differences have a role within a specified model and the permissible
transformations are more restricted. A cardinal interpretation still does not,
by itself, justify comparisons of utility across different people. In an
ordinal framework, we cannot say that a bundle giving utility $20$ makes a
consumer twice as well off as a bundle giving utility $10$. We can say only
that the former is preferred.

The ordinal interpretation is important in welfare analysis. Comparing utility
numbers across different individuals requires additional ethical assumptions.
Economics can describe choices and trade-offs, but interpersonal comparisons
of well-being cannot be read mechanically from a utility function. This is why
a policy calculation needs a stated view about fairness as well as a model of
behaviour.

\begin{keyidea}
\textbf{PPE focus: utility numbers do not settle public policy.} If a policy
changes Ana's utility from $10$ to $20$ and Bruno's from $100$ to $110$, the
numbers do not show whose gain is larger. Each person's utility scale could be
represented differently while preserving the same rankings. A social judgement
therefore requires an explicit ethical principle, not only a utility
calculation.
\end{keyidea}

\section{Indifference curves}

An \keyterm{indifference curve} is the set of all bundles that give a
consumer the same utility. In everyday language, it joins combinations the
consumer regards as equally good. For $u(x,y)=xy$, the indifference curve
associated with utility level $\bar{u}$ is
\[
xy=\bar{u},
\]
or equivalently
\[
y=\frac{\bar{u}}{x}.
\]

For instance, the bundles $(2,6)$, $(3,4)$, and $(4,3)$ all lie on the
indifference curve $xy=12$. The consumer is indifferent among them because
each provides the same utility. You might imagine different weekly
combinations of meals out and home-cooked meals that feel equally satisfactory
to a particular student.

Indifference curves have three important properties, each supported by a
different assumption:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item with strongly monotonic preferences, they slope downward: receiving less of one good requires more of the other to maintain utility;
  \item with strongly monotonic preferences, curves farther to the north-east represent preferred bundles;
  \item distinct curves do not cross, because one bundle cannot be assigned two different utility levels.
\end{enumerate}
Convexity determines curvature: convex preferences give weakly bowed-in curves,
whereas strict convexity gives strictly bowed-in curves. Perfect substitutes
are the useful boundary case: their preferences are convex, but their
indifference curves are straight lines.

\section{Marginal utility}

The \keyterm{marginal utility} of a good is the additional utility associated
with a small increase in its quantity, holding the quantity of the other good
fixed. The word marginal always directs attention to the next small unit, not
to the total amount. For a two-good utility function $u(x,y)$,
\[
MU_x=\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
MU_y=\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}.
\]

For $u(x,y)=xy$,
\[
MU_x=y
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
MU_y=x.
\]
An additional unit of $x$ is more valuable when the consumer has more $y$,
because the two goods complement each other in the utility function. For
example, an extra serving of sauce may be more useful when there is more pasta
to eat it with.

\section{The marginal rate of substitution}

The \keyterm{marginal rate of substitution} of $x$ for $y$, written
$MRS_{x,y}$, measures the amount of $y$ a consumer is willing to give up for a
very small additional amount of $x$ at the current bundle, while remaining on
the same indifference curve. It is a local trade-off: the answer can differ
when the consumer starts with a different bundle. At a smooth point where
$MU_y>0$, utility is held fixed:
\[
0=du=MU_x\,dx+MU_y\,dy.
\]
Hence the slope and MRS are
\[
\left.\frac{dy}{dx}\right|_{\bar u}
=-\frac{MU_x}{MU_y},
\qquad
MRS_{x,y}
=-\left.\frac{dy}{dx}\right|_{\bar u}
=\frac{MU_x}{MU_y}.
\]

For $u(x,y)=xy$,
\[
MRS_{x,y}=\frac{y}{x}.
\]
At a bundle with much $y$ and little $x$, the consumer is willing to give up a
relatively large amount of $y$ for another unit of $x$. As $x$ becomes more
abundant and $y$ scarcer, the willingness to substitute falls. This is the
\keyterm{diminishing marginal rate of substitution}, which produces bowed-in
indifference curves.

\begin{keyidea}
\textbf{Watch the sign.} The slope of an indifference curve is negative. By
convention, the MRS is reported as a positive number:
\[
\left.\frac{dy}{dx}\right|_{\bar u}=-MRS_{x,y}.
\]
The formula applies only at smooth points with positive marginal utilities. At
the kink of a perfect-complements indifference curve, there is no unique slope
and no single MRS.
\end{keyidea}

\begin{exercisebox}
\textbf{Worked example: diminishing MRS along one curve.} On the indifference
curve $xy=12$,
\[
y=\frac{12}{x}
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
MRS_{x,y}=\frac{y}{x}=\frac{12}{x^2}.
\]
Thus, at $(2,6)$, $(3,4)$, and $(4,3)$, the MRS values are respectively
$3$, $4/3$, and $3/4$. As $x$ rises along the same indifference curve, the
consumer gives up less $y$ for an additional unit of $x$.
\end{exercisebox}

Diminishing MRS is not the same claim as diminishing marginal utility; it
concerns the ratio of marginal utilities along an indifference curve.

\section{Common preference representations}

\subsection{Cobb--Douglas preferences}

The utility function
\[
u(x,y)=x^\alpha y^\beta,
\]
where $\alpha>0$ and $\beta>0$, is called \keyterm{Cobb--Douglas}. It
represents smooth, downward-sloping, convex indifference curves. This is the
workhorse case when we want a consumer who values both goods and likes
variety. Its marginal rate of substitution is
\[
MRS_{x,y}=\frac{\alpha}{\beta}\frac{y}{x}.
\]
The exponents influence the relative importance of the two goods. Only the
ratio $\alpha/\beta$ affects the ordinal preference ranking and the MRS.
Multiplying both exponents by the same positive number changes the utility
representation but not the underlying preferences. For example,
$x^{1/3}y^{2/3}$ and $x^{2/3}y^{4/3}$ represent the same ordinal
preferences.

\subsection{Perfect substitutes}

If two goods are perfect substitutes, the consumer is willing to replace one
with the other at a constant rate. Two brands of bottled water may be close
to this case for a consumer who cares only about the amount of water. A
utility function is
\[
u(x,y)=x+2y.
\]
Its indifference curves are straight lines. Here, one additional unit of $y$ gives the same utility as two additional units of $x$.

\subsection{Perfect complements}

If goods are valuable only when consumed in fixed proportions, they are
perfect complements. For example,
\[
u(x,y)=\min\{x,y\}
\]
can represent left and right shoes. The indifference curves are L-shaped. An
additional left shoe raises utility only while left shoes are scarce relative
to right shoes. Once the consumer has at least as many left shoes as right
shoes, further left shoes without additional right shoes do not increase
utility.

\subsection{Quasilinear preferences}

The utility function
\[
u(x,y)=v(x)+y
\]
is called \keyterm{quasilinear}. Its marginal utility of $y$ is constant,
while the marginal utility of $x$ may diminish. Such preferences are useful
in analysing choices where one good is a general ``outside good,'' such as
money spent on other consumption. For instance, $x$ might be visits to a
museum and $y$ all other spending.

When $v$ is differentiable,
\[
MU_x=v'(x),
\qquad
MU_y=1,
\qquad
MRS_{x,y}=v'(x).
\]
Its indifference curves are vertical translations of one another:
\[
y=\bar{u}-v(x).
\]

\section{What indifference curves do not say}

Indifference curves are not psychological maps. They do not imply that
consumers consciously calculate marginal utilities, and they do not show how
preferences were formed. They provide a compact way to represent observed or
hypothetical rankings. Their value lies in making the trade-offs implied by
those rankings visible.

Indifference curves describe the trade-offs a consumer is willing to make. A
budget line will describe the trade-offs the market requires. The next chapter
puts these two parts together: what someone wants, and what their income and
prices allow.

\section*{Chapter Review}

\subsection*{Key concepts}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Utility function}: a numerical representation of a preference ranking.
  \item \textbf{Ordinal utility}: utility numbers rank bundles but do not measure well-being in absolute units.
  \item \textbf{Indifference curve}: the set of bundles giving the same utility.
  \item \textbf{Marginal utility}: the change in utility from a small increase in one good.
  \item \textbf{Marginal rate of substitution}: the trade-off a consumer accepts while keeping utility unchanged.
  \item \textbf{Diminishing MRS}: willingness to substitute falls as a consumer has more of one good relative to the other.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Key equations}
\[
MU_x=\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}, \qquad
MU_y=\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}, \qquad
MRS_{x,y}=\frac{MU_x}{MU_y}
=-\left.\frac{dy}{dx}\right|_{\bar u}.
\]

\section*{Exercises}

\subsection*{Conceptual questions}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.]
  \item What does it mean for utility to be ordinal? Why can we replace $u(x,y)$ with $100u(x,y)+7$ without changing preferences? Does $v(x,y)=-xy$ represent the same preferences as $u(x,y)=xy$? Explain.
  \item State and explain the three standard properties of indifference curves.
  \item Define marginal utility and the marginal rate of substitution.
  \item Explain why indifference curves do not normally cross.
  \item Give one example each of perfect substitutes and perfect complements.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Graphical exercises}
\begin{enumerate}[resume,label=\arabic*.]
  \item For $u(x,y)=xy$, first rewrite the indifference curves $u=6$, $u=12$, and $u=18$ in the form $y=\bar{u}/x$. Then draw them and mark the bundles $(2,3)$, $(3,4)$, and $(6,3)$.
  \item Draw representative indifference curves for perfect substitutes and perfect complements. Explain their different shapes.
  \item For $u(x,y)=x+2y$, write an equation for the indifference curve through $(4,3)$, calculate its slope and MRS, and draw two curves.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Analytical exercises}
\begin{enumerate}[resume,label=\arabic*.]
  \item Let $u(x,y)=x^{1/3}y^{2/3}$. Calculate $MU_x$, $MU_y$, and $MRS_{x,y}$.
  \item A consumer has utility $u(x,y)=xy+2x$. Find the equation of the indifference curve through $(3,4)$.
  \item For $u(x,y)=\min\{2x,y\}$, describe the bundles on the indifference curve associated with utility $10$.
  \item Let $u(x,y)=xy$ and $v(x,y)=e^{xy}$. Show that $v$ represents the same ordinal preferences as $u$. Calculate the MRS under both representations.
  \item On the indifference curve $xy=24$, calculate the MRS at $(2,12)$, $(4,6)$, and $(6,4)$. Explain the pattern.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Exam-style multiple-choice questions}
\begin{enumerate}[resume,label=\arabic*.]
  \item If two utility functions rank every pair of bundles in the same order, then: (a) they represent different preferences; (b) they represent the same ordinal preferences; (c) they must assign identical utility values; or (d) neither can be used in consumer theory.
  \item If $u(x,y)=xy$, the marginal utility of $x$ is: (a) $x$; (b) $y$; (c) $xy$; or (d) $y/x$.
  \item Perfect-complement preferences are represented by indifference curves that are: (a) straight lines; (b) upward sloping; (c) L-shaped; or (d) horizontal.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent Does an ordinal account of preferences provide enough information to compare social outcomes? Discuss the distinction between identifying a person's own ranking of options and making interpersonal comparisons of well-being.
