\chapter{Cost Curves and Supply}
\label{chap:cost-curves}

\section{Learning objectives}

After completing this chapter, you should be able to:
\begin{itemize}
  \item distinguish fixed, variable, total, average, and marginal costs;
  \item explain the shapes and relationships of standard short-run cost curves;
  \item apply the shutdown rule and distinguish it from long-run exit; and
  \item derive the short-run supply curve of a competitive firm.
\end{itemize}

\section{From technology to costs}

Chapter~\ref{chap:cost-choice} asked which inputs a firm uses to produce a
given output at the lowest possible cost. This chapter begins with the result
of that choice: a \keyterm{cost function}, which gives the minimum cost of
producing each feasible output at given technology and input prices. It lets
us move from the question of how the firm should make an output to the question
of what one more unit costs. A firm first faces a technological
problem---how inputs produce output---and then a cost problem---which of the
feasible input combinations is least expensive.

The distinction between the \keyterm{short run} and the long run is a
decision-period distinction, not a calendar definition. In the short run, at
least one input cannot be changed. In the long run, all inputs can be varied.
An oven that cannot be sold or resized before tonight's service is fixed for
tonight, even if it can be replaced next year.

\section{Short-run costs}

In the short run, some inputs are fixed. \keyterm{Fixed cost} ($FC$) does not
vary with output: rent on a lease is a common example. \keyterm{Variable
cost} ($VC(q)$) changes with output, such as the cost of ingredients or
hourly labour. Asking whether a cost changes when the firm makes one more
unit is often the quickest way to classify it. Total cost is
\[
TC(q)=FC+VC(q).
\]

\begin{keyidea}
\textbf{The cost family.} For positive output,
\[
TC=FC+VC,\qquad
ATC=\frac{TC}{q}=AFC+AVC,\qquad
MC=\frac{dTC}{dq}=\frac{dVC}{dq}.
\]
Average costs are defined only when $q>0$. Fixed cost affects total and
average cost, but not marginal cost: it does not change when one additional
unit is produced.
\end{keyidea}

Fixed cost is paid even when output is zero; variable cost is zero when no
variable inputs are used. The distinction is about whether a cost can be
avoided by producing nothing in the relevant period, not about whether a
payment is large or small.

\begin{exercisebox}
\textbf{An evening order at a bakery.}
At 5 p.m., a bakery has already committed to a rented oven and a manager's
shift. Those payments are fixed for the evening. Flour, packaging, and the
hours of additional temporary staff needed for a late order are variable.
The bakery should ask whether accepting the order covers its \emph{additional}
costs. The unavoidable oven rent matters for its overall profit, but it does
not by itself decide whether one more tray should be baked tonight.
\end{exercisebox}

\section{Average and marginal costs}

Average fixed cost, average variable cost, and average total cost are
\[
AFC=\frac{FC}{q}, \qquad
AVC=\frac{VC(q)}{q}, \qquad
ATC=\frac{TC(q)}{q}=AFC+AVC.
\]
Marginal cost is the additional cost of producing one additional unit:
\[
MC=\frac{dTC}{dq}=\frac{dVC}{dq}.
\]

Average fixed cost always falls as output rises, because a fixed amount is
spread over more units. Average variable cost and average total cost are often
U-shaped. At low output, specialisation can reduce average cost; at higher
output, diminishing marginal product raises the variable input needed for
extra output. A food truck, for instance, may use its first staff members and
equipment more fully as it gets busier, but a very long queue eventually makes
each extra order harder to prepare.

The relationship between marginal and average cost is not merely a diagram
to memorise. Differentiating the two averages gives
\[
\frac{dAVC}{dq}=\frac{MC-AVC}{q},
\qquad
\frac{dATC}{dq}=\frac{MC-ATC}{q}.
\]
Because $q>0$, an average falls when marginal cost is below it and rises when
marginal cost is above it. Thus MC crosses both AVC and ATC at their minimum
points, as Figure~\ref{fig:standard-cost-curves} illustrates.

\section{The competitive firm's output choice}

A competitive firm is a \keyterm{price taker}: it can sell as much as it
wishes at the market price but cannot influence that price. A single wheat
farmer in a large grain market is a useful approximation: changing one farm's
output does not change the market price. This is an assumption about the firm,
not a claim that every real firm is small or powerless. For a price-taking
firm, total revenue is $TR=pq$, so
\[
MR=\frac{dTR}{dq}=p.
\]
The firm chooses output where $p=MC$, provided it is worthwhile to operate.
This condition identifies an interior candidate; the firm must also compare
it with shutting down and respect $q\geq0$.

\section{The shutdown decision}

In the short run, fixed costs are unavoidable. The relevant comparison is
therefore between the revenue from operating and the costs that operating
adds. A firm should produce if revenue can cover variable cost, or equivalently
if price is at least minimum average variable cost:
\[
p\geq\min AVC.
\]
If $p<\min AVC$, each unit produced adds more variable cost than revenue, so
shutting down minimises the loss. If $\min AVC<p<\min ATC$, the firm produces
but makes an \keyterm{operating loss}; it covers variable cost and contributes
something toward fixed cost. At exactly $p=\min AVC$, the firm is indifferent
between shutting down and producing the output at the minimum of AVC.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{p{0.25\textwidth}p{0.27\textwidth}p{0.35\textwidth}}
\toprule
Price relative to cost & Short-run choice & Interpretation \\
\midrule
$p<\min AVC$ & Shut down: $q=0$ & Producing would make the loss larger. \\
$p=\min AVC$ & Indifferent at the shutdown point & Revenue just covers variable cost. \\
$\min AVC<p<\min ATC$ & Produce where $p=MC$ & The firm has a loss, but it is smaller than fixed cost. \\
$p\geq\min ATC$ & Produce where $p=MC$ & Profit is non-negative at or above the break-even price. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The \keyterm{shutdown decision} is therefore different from the \keyterm{exit
decision}. A shutdown asks whether to produce in the current short run, when
fixed cost cannot be avoided. Exit asks whether to remain in the market in the
long run, when inputs can be adjusted. The forward-looking test is whether
expected revenue covers total avoidable costs, or equivalently for a
price-taking firm whether expected price covers the relevant minimum long-run
average cost. Past sunk costs remain irrelevant to that decision.

\section{Supply}

The competitive firm's short-run supply curve is the portion of its
marginal-cost curve above the minimum of AVC. It is a summary of the
quantities the firm would choose at different possible market prices, rather
than a description of what buyers demand. At any price above this threshold,
the firm chooses the quantity where $p=MC$
(Figure~\ref{fig:firm-supply}). At the threshold itself, the firm is
indifferent; the conventional supply curve records zero output at that
endpoint.

\FloatBarrier

This rule has a clear scope. It applies to a competitive, price-taking firm.
A monopolist chooses output using marginal revenue rather than price, and does
not have an analogous supply curve determined by price alone. Market supply is
the horizontal sum of the individual supply curves of competitive firms.

\section{Worked example: cost, shutdown, and supply}

Suppose a competitive firm's short-run cost function is
\[
C(q)=100+4q+q^2.
\]
Then
\[
FC=100,\qquad VC(q)=4q+q^2,\qquad AVC(q)=4+q,
\]
and
\[
MC(q)=4+2q.
\]
The lowest possible AVC is \euro4 per unit (approached as output tends to
zero), so the shutdown price is \euro4. For a market price above that point,
the firm sets $p=MC$:
\[
p=4+2q
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
q^s(p)=\frac{p-4}{2}.
\]
Thus its short-run supply is
\[
q^s(p)=
\begin{cases}
0 & \text{if } p\leq4,\\[2pt]
\dfrac{p-4}{2} & \text{if } p>4.
\end{cases}
\]
At $p=20$, the firm produces $q=8$. Revenue is \euro160 and total cost is
\euro196, so profit is $-\euro36$. It nevertheless produces: shutting down
would lose the whole \euro100 fixed cost, whereas producing covers all
variable cost and part of it. This is the key short-run lesson: a loss does
not automatically imply shutdown. The profit identity makes the comparison concise:
\[
\pi=q[p-ATC(q)].
\]

\section*{Chapter Review}

\subsection*{Key concepts and equations}
\begin{itemize}
  \item $TC=FC+VC$ and $ATC=AFC+AVC$.
  \item $MC=dTC/dq=dVC/dq$; average costs require $q>0$.
  \item $MC$ crosses $AVC$ and $ATC$ at their minimum points.
  \item A competitive firm chooses $p=MC$ when operating, subject to
  feasibility and the shutdown comparison.
  \item The short-run shutdown condition is $p\geq\min AVC$; long-run exit
  requires considering $ATC$.
  \item The short-run supply curve is the MC curve above minimum AVC.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Exercises}

\subsection*{Conceptual questions}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.]
  \item Distinguish fixed and variable cost. Give two examples of each for a restaurant.
  \item A theatre has paid for tonight's building rental. Classify the extra
  cleaning staff, programmes, and the rental payment as fixed or variable for
  the decision to stage one additional performance.
  \item Why does marginal cost not depend on fixed cost?
  \item Explain why MC crosses AVC and ATC at their minimum points. Use the
  derivative relationship or the “marginal pulls the average” intuition.
  \item Distinguish the short-run shutdown decision from the long-run exit decision.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Graphical exercises}
\begin{enumerate}[resume,label=\arabic*.]
  \item Draw AFC, AVC, ATC, and MC curves with their standard shapes. Mark the
  minimum points of AVC and ATC and explain the two MC intersections.
  \item On the same diagram, show a competitive firm facing three prices: one
  below minimum AVC, one between minimum AVC and ATC, and one above ATC. State
  the firm's output and profit sign in each case.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Analytical exercises}
\begin{enumerate}[resume,label=\arabic*.]
  \item A firm's cost function is $C(q)=100+4q+q^2$. Find $FC$, $VC$, $AVC$,
  $ATC$, and $MC$.
  \item For the firm in Exercise 8, find the output chosen when market price is
  $20$. Does the firm produce when price is $3$? Explain using the shutdown rule.
  \item A competitive firm faces $p=15$ and has $TC(q)=50+3q+q^2$. Calculate
  output, revenue, total cost, and profit. Does it operate in the short run?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Exam-style multiple-choice questions}
\begin{enumerate}[resume,label=\arabic*.]
  \item A competitive firm's short-run supply curve is: (a) its demand curve;
  (b) its MC curve above minimum AVC; (c) its ATC curve; or (d) its fixed-cost curve.
  \item A firm should shut down in the short run if: (a) $p<ATC$;
  (b) $p<\min AVC$; (c) $p<MC$ at every output; or (d) fixed cost is positive.
  \item If marginal cost is below average total cost, producing an additional
  unit will: (a) raise ATC; (b) lower ATC; (c) leave ATC unchanged; or
  (d) make fixed cost zero.
\end{enumerate}
