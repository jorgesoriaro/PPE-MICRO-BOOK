\chapter{Consumers and Preferences}
\label{chap:preferences}

\section{Learning objectives}

After completing this chapter, you should be able to:
\begin{itemize}
  \item describe a consumer's choice problem using consumption bundles;
  \item use the symbols $\succeq$, $\succ$, and $\sim$ correctly;
  \item state and interpret the principal axioms of rational preferences;
  \item explain monotonicity, convexity, and their graphical implications; and
  \item recognise both the usefulness and the limits of the rational-choice model.
\end{itemize}

\section{Choice theory}

Consumer theory begins with a simple question: how does a person choose among the alternatives available to them? The answer will eventually depend on income and prices, but first we need a way to describe what the person likes or values. This is the role of \keyterm{preferences}.

The standard model does not assume that every consumer likes the same things, nor that consumers care only about material goods. A person may value leisure, friendship, health, environmental quality, status, or ethical consumption. What the model assumes is that, when faced with alternatives, the consumer can compare them in a sufficiently consistent way to make a choice.

\section{Consumption bundles}

Suppose that a consumer chooses quantities of two goods, $x$ and $y$. A \keyterm{consumption bundle} is an ordered pair $(x,y)$ describing the quantities consumed. For example, $(3,5)$ may mean three units of food and five units of clothing.

Using two goods is a simplification, but it is powerful. It allows us to draw choices on a diagram and to focus on the trade-off between alternatives. Later, $x$ and $y$ can stand for broad categories such as present consumption and future consumption, private goods and environmental quality, or leisure and income.

\section{Describing preferences}

Let $A$ and $B$ denote two bundles. The notation
\[
A\succeq B
\]
means that the consumer considers $A$ \term{at least as good as} $B$. It includes two possibilities: the consumer may strictly prefer $A$, or may be indifferent between $A$ and $B$.

The notation
\[
A\succ B
\]
means that $A$ is \keyterm{strictly preferred} to $B$. The consumer chooses $A$ over $B$ when both are available. Finally,
\[
A\sim B
\]
means that the consumer is \keyterm{indifferent} between the two bundles: each is considered at least as good as the other.

Preferences rank alternatives. They do not yet explain \emph{why} one bundle is preferred. The reasons may be personal, cultural, moral, or social. The economic model works with the ranking itself.

\section{Rational preferences}

In introductory microeconomics, preferences are called \keyterm{rational} if they satisfy a set of consistency properties. Rationality here does not mean selfishness, perfect information, or moral superiority. It means that preferences can be represented coherently.

\subsection{Completeness}

Preferences are \keyterm{complete} if, for any two bundles $A$ and $B$, the consumer can compare them: either $A\succeq B$ or $B\succeq A$, or both. The consumer need not have a strong opinion, but ``I cannot compare these options at all'' is excluded.

Completeness is a useful approximation. In real life, people may face unfamiliar alternatives or may postpone a decision. The assumption becomes more plausible when the alternatives are well understood and the consumer has enough time to decide.

\subsection{Transitivity}

Preferences are \keyterm{transitive} if
\[
A\succeq B \quad \text{and} \quad B\succeq C
\quad \Longrightarrow \quad A\succeq C.
\]
Transitivity rules out cycles of preference. If a consumer preferred $A$ to $B$, $B$ to $C$, and $C$ to $A$, a clever seller could repeatedly exchange bundles and leave the consumer worse off. Transitivity therefore protects the idea that choice is consistent over comparable alternatives.

\subsection{Reflexivity}

Preferences are \keyterm{reflexive} if every bundle is at least as good as itself:
\[
A\succeq A.
\]
This property is usually taken for granted, but it is necessary for a complete formal description of preferences.

\section{More is better: monotonicity}

The assumption of \keyterm{monotonicity}, also called non-satiation, states that more of a good is preferred to less, holding everything else constant. If $A=(x_A,y_A)$ and $B=(x_B,y_B)$, with $x_A\geq x_B$ and $y_A\geq y_B$, and at least one inequality strict, then monotonicity implies $A\succ B$.

For ordinary goods, this is often a reasonable starting point. It implies that a consumer will not voluntarily throw away a desirable good. However, some goods can become ``bads'' beyond a certain point: pollution, noise, risk, or even food after satiation. The assumption must therefore be matched to the question. We can define a good so that larger quantities are desirable within the relevant range, or use a model that explicitly allows satiation.

\section{Continuity and convexity}

\keyterm{Continuity} means that small changes in a bundle do not normally generate abrupt jumps in preferences. Technically, if a sequence of bundles approaches a bundle that is at least as good as another, then sufficiently close bundles should also be at least as good. Continuity helps ensure that preferences can be represented by a utility function.

\keyterm{Convexity} captures a preference for variety or balanced bundles. If a consumer is indifferent between $A$ and $B$, then a mixture of the two is at least as good as either one. With \keyterm{strict convexity}, a genuine mixture is strictly preferred to either extreme.

For example, a student may prefer a balanced allocation of meals and leisure to an extreme allocation that gives only one of them. Convexity does not say that every consumer likes every mixture; it says that extreme bundles are not systematically preferred merely because they are extreme. The assumption will imply the familiar bowed-in shape of indifference curves.

\section{From preferences to choice}

Preferences alone do not determine an observed choice. A consumer may prefer a luxury holiday to a local holiday but lack the income to buy it. Choice requires both preferences and a \keyterm{feasible set}: the alternatives that are actually available. In the next chapter, utility functions and indifference curves will represent preferences; after that, budget constraints will describe the feasible set.

The model is deliberately modest. It describes what a person chooses when their preferences and constraints are given. It does not claim that preferences are fixed forever or unaffected by social context. Advertising, habit, family, institutions, social norms, and political power can shape the alternatives people perceive and the preferences they express.

\section*{Chapter Review}

\subsection*{Key concepts}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Consumption bundle}: a list of quantities of goods consumed.
  \item \textbf{Preference relation}: a ranking of alternative bundles.
  \item \textbf{Completeness}: every pair of bundles can be compared.
  \item \textbf{Transitivity}: preference rankings do not form cycles.
  \item \textbf{Monotonicity}: more of a good is preferred to less, other things equal.
  \item \textbf{Continuity}: small changes do not cause arbitrary jumps in preferences.
  \item \textbf{Convexity}: mixtures of equally valued bundles are at least as good as extremes.
  \item \textbf{Feasible set}: the alternatives available to the consumer.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Exercises}

\subsection*{Conceptual questions}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.]
  \item Define a consumption bundle. Why are two-good models useful even though consumers purchase many goods?
  \item Explain the difference between $A\succeq B$, $A\succ B$, and $A\sim B$.
  \item State completeness and transitivity precisely. Give one example of a situation in which each assumption may be a useful approximation.
  \item Does rationality in consumer theory mean selfishness? Explain.
  \item Explain why monotonicity may be inappropriate for pollution or risk.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Graphical exercises}
\begin{enumerate}[resume,label=\arabic*.]
  \item In a diagram with good $x$ on the horizontal axis and good $y$ on the vertical axis, place the bundles $A=(2,2)$, $B=(4,2)$, $C=(3,5)$, and $D=(6,6)$. Assuming monotonicity, compare $A$ with $B$, $A$ with $C$, and $A$ with $D$. Can you compare $B$ and $C$ without more information?
  \item Draw two bundles that illustrate convex preferences. Explain why their midpoint is weakly preferred to each endpoint.
  \item Draw a case in which monotonicity holds and identify the direction in which preferred bundles lie.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Analytical exercises}
\begin{enumerate}[resume,label=\arabic*.]
  \item Suppose $A\succ B$, $B\sim C$, and $C\succeq D$. What can transitivity tell us about the relation between $A$ and $D$?
  \item A consumer has the following rankings: $A\succ B$, $B\succ C$, and $C\succ A$. Does this consumer satisfy transitivity? Explain the practical problem caused by this pattern.
  \item Let $A=(4,3)$ and $B=(2,3)$. Under monotonicity, write the relevant preference relation and justify it.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Exam-style multiple-choice questions}
\begin{enumerate}[resume,label=\arabic*.]
  \item If $A\sim B$, then: (a) $A\succ B$; (b) $B\succ A$; (c) $A\succeq B$ and $B\succeq A$; or (d) the consumer cannot compare $A$ and $B$.
  \item Transitivity means that if $A\succeq B$ and $B\succeq C$, then: (a) $C\succeq A$; (b) $A\succeq C$; (c) $A\sim C$; or (d) $B\succ A$.
  \item Convex preferences imply that, when a consumer is indifferent between two bundles: (a) only extreme bundles can be chosen; (b) a mixture is at least as good as either bundle; (c) more is always worse; or (d) the consumer has no preferences.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{PPE discussion question}

\noindent Consumer theory treats preferences as given. Discuss whether this is an acceptable simplification for public policy. In your answer, consider advertising, education, social norms, and the possibility that preferences themselves may be shaped by institutions.
