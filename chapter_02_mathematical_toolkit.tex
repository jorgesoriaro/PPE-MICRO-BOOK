\chapter{Mathematical and Graphical Toolkit}
\label{chap:maths}

\section{Learning objectives}

After completing this chapter, you should be able to:
\begin{itemize}
  \item solve the linear equations used in introductory microeconomics;
  \item interpret functions, graphs, slopes, and intercepts;
  \item calculate and interpret ordinary and partial derivatives; and
  \item use marginal analysis to identify an optimum.
\end{itemize}

\section{Why mathematics matters in microeconomics}

Microeconomics uses mathematics to make an argument precise. A diagram can
show that a higher price is associated with a lower quantity demanded; an
equation says exactly how the two variables are related. A derivative tells us
how quickly an outcome changes when one variable changes. Mathematics is not
an end in itself. It is a language for describing choices, constraints, and
trade-offs---and a way to check that our verbal reasoning is consistent.

\begin{keyidea}
\textbf{Mathematics is a translation tool.} In this course, the same economic
idea should be readable in three forms:
\[
\text{words} \quad \longleftrightarrow \quad \text{equation}
\quad \longleftrightarrow \quad \text{graph}.
\]
If one form feels difficult, move to another and then return. Ask what the
economic question is, what equation represents it, and what the relationship
looks like in a diagram.
\end{keyidea}

\section{Variables, parameters, and functions}

A \keyterm{variable} is a quantity that can take different values. In
consumer theory, $x$ and $y$ may denote quantities of two goods; in a market,
$p$ may denote price and $q$ quantity. A \keyterm{parameter} is treated as
fixed within a particular problem, even though it may differ across
situations. For example, income is held fixed while we ask how a change in
the price of coffee affects a student's purchases. In another question,
income itself may be the variable of interest.

A \keyterm{function} describes how one variable depends on one or more
others. You can think of it as a rule that takes an input and returns an
output. For example,
\[
q(p)=100-2p
\]
is a demand function. It states that quantity demanded $q$ depends on price
$p$. The notation $q(p)$ says that price is the input and quantity is the
output: for example, $q(20)=60$. The parentheses do not mean multiplication;
they ask the rule for the value produced when $p=20$. An equation can
describe a relationship, while functional notation highlights which quantity
the model is currently treating as the output.

\begin{exercisebox}
\textbf{Example: a mobile-data plan.} Suppose a plan costs
\[
B(g)=15+2g,
\]
where $g$ is the number of extra gigabytes used in a month. The intercept,
\euro15, is the fixed monthly charge. The slope, \euro2 per gigabyte, is the
additional charge for one more gigabyte. Reading the formula with its units
makes the algebra economically meaningful.
\end{exercisebox}

\section{Linear equations, slopes, and intercepts}

Many introductory models use linear equations:
\[
y=a+bx,
\]
where $a$ is the vertical intercept and $b$ is the slope. The intercept is the
value of $y$ when $x=0$. The slope measures the change in $y$ associated with
a one-unit increase in $x$. A taxi fare with a fixed starting charge plus a
charge per kilometre has exactly this form.

The direct demand function above can also be written in inverse form:
\[
q=100-2p
\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
p=50-\frac{1}{2}q.
\]
In the inverse form, when $q=0$, price is \euro50, so the vertical intercept
is $50$. The slope is $-1/2$: an additional unit sold is associated with a
price \euro0.50 lower. Setting $p=0$ gives $q=100$, the horizontal intercept.

Demand can be written in direct form, $q=D(p)$, or inverse form, $p=P(q)$.
The numerical slope depends on which variable is on the vertical axis. In
this example, the direct-demand slope is $-2$ units per euro, whereas the
inverse-demand slope is $-1/2$ euros per unit. This can feel like two
different answers at first, but the two equations describe the same
relationship from two useful directions.

\paragraph{Domains and units.} An equation is not automatically an
economically meaningful model at every number. Quantities cannot normally be
negative, prices require units, and a relationship may apply only within a
stated range. For the inverse demand curve above, the economically relevant
range is $0\leq q\leq100$. The slope is not merely $-1/2$: it is minus
\euro0.50 per additional unit of output.

To solve an equation, isolate the unknown. If $300-5x=0$, then $5x=300$, and
therefore $x=60$. Think of this as undoing operations one at a time. Check an
answer by substituting it into the original equation.

The slope between two points is
\[
\text{slope}=\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}.
\]
For a straight line, the slope is the same everywhere. A positive slope indicates that $y$ increases when $x$ increases; a negative slope indicates that $y$ decreases when $x$ increases.

\section{Solving two linear equations}

Market equilibrium, later chapters on oligopoly, and many policy examples
require two equations to hold at once. At a competitive equilibrium, for
example, quantity demanded equals quantity supplied. The task is simply to
find the price that makes both sides describe the same quantity. If
\[
Q_D=80-2p
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
Q_S=20+p,
\]
then equilibrium requires
\[
80-2p=20+p.
\]
Collecting the price terms gives $60=3p$, so $p^*=20$. Substituting this
price into either original equation gives $Q^*=40$. The star indicates an
equilibrium value. The final substitution is an important check: both sides
must give the same quantity.

\section{Graphs and economic diagrams}

A graph is a visual representation of a relationship. In the usual
convention, the horizontal axis measures an independent variable and the
vertical axis a dependent variable. These labels describe the presentation;
they do not automatically establish causation. In a market-equilibrium model,
price and quantity are jointly determined. Economics nevertheless usually
places quantity on the horizontal axis and price on the vertical axis because
this makes market diagrams easier to read and compare.

When reading a graph, first identify the variables and units on both axes.
Then ask a simple question: did something on an axis change, or did another
factor change the relationship itself? A price change produces a movement
along a demand curve; a change in income, tastes, or the price of a related
good may shift the curve. This distinction will return throughout the book.

\section{Derivatives and marginal change}

An average rate of change compares two points:
\[
\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}.
\]
The \keyterm{derivative} of a function measures the instantaneous rate of
change: the limit of this average rate as $h$ becomes very small. Formal
limit calculations are not required for most of this book. The important
intuition is that derivatives describe what happens at the margin. If $f(x)$
is an economic outcome, the derivative $f'(x)$, or $\frac{df}{dx}$, measures
how $f$ changes when $x$ changes by a very small amount. It answers questions
such as: how much extra cost is associated with producing one more unit at
the current output level?

For a power function,
\[
\frac{d}{dx}x^n=nx^{n-1}.
\]
Thus,
\[
\frac{d}{dx}x^2=2x
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{3}{4}x^3+7x^2-5x\right)=\frac{9}{4}x^2+14x-5.
\]
Two useful companion rules are
\[
\frac{d}{dx}c=0,
\qquad
\frac{d}{dx}\bigl[af(x)+bg(x)\bigr]=af'(x)+bg'(x),
\]
where $a$, $b$, and $c$ are constants. Fractional powers appear often in
utility and production functions. For positive $x$,
\[
x^{1/2}=\sqrt{x},
\qquad
x^{-1/2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{x}},
\qquad
\frac{d}{dx}x^\alpha=\alpha x^{\alpha-1}.
\]

In economics, derivatives are usually called \keyterm{marginal} values. If
$C(q)$ is total cost, then $C'(q)$ is marginal cost: approximately the
additional cost of producing one more unit. If $U(x)$ is utility, then
$U'(x)$ is marginal utility. The word marginal should therefore make you ask:
what changes if we take one small additional step?

\section{Partial derivatives}

Economic choices often involve more than one variable. A utility function may
be $u(x,y)$, where $x$ and $y$ are two goods. A \keyterm{partial derivative}
measures the effect of changing one variable while holding the other variables
fixed. It is the mathematical version of asking how one ingredient changes a
recipe while leaving the others untouched.

For
\[
f(x,y)=2x^2+3xy+y^2-5,
\]
the partial derivative with respect to $x$ is
\[
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}=4x+3y.
\]
When differentiating with respect to $x$, treat $y$ as a constant. Thus,
\[
\frac{\partial(xy)}{\partial x}=y,
\qquad
\frac{\partial(y^2)}{\partial x}=0.
\]
Use $d$ for a one-variable function and $\partial$ when a function has
several independently varying arguments. The partial derivative with respect
to $y$ is
\[
\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}=3x+2y.
\]

\section{Optimisation}

Many microeconomic problems ask an agent to choose the best feasible option.
The structure is always similar:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item identify the objective, such as maximising utility or profit;
  \item identify the constraint, such as a budget or available technology;
  \item express the objective in terms of the choice variable or variables;
  \item find candidate optima using a marginal condition; and
  \item check that the candidate is feasible and is a maximum rather than a minimum.
\end{enumerate}

For a differentiable objective with an interior solution, a first-order
condition identifies a \emph{candidate} optimum, not a conclusion. It tells
us where to look first; boundary points and the shape of the objective still
matter.

Consider a student's net benefit from $h$ hours of revision:
\[
F(h)=20h-2h^2,
\qquad 0\leq h\leq10.
\]
The derivative is
\[
\frac{dF}{dh}=20-4h.
\]
Setting it equal to zero gives the candidate $h=5$. Intuitively, the next
hour is beneficial before this point and costly after it. Since
\[
\frac{d^2F}{dh^2}=-4<0
\]
throughout the feasible interval, $F$ is strictly concave and the candidate
is the unique global maximum. The boundary values are $F(0)=F(10)=0$, while
$F(5)=50$, confirming the result.

\begin{keyidea}
\textbf{Four checks before you finish a calculation.} Check the signs; state
the units; verify the domain and any constraint; and decide whether a
stationary point is a maximum, a minimum, or a boundary case. Correct algebra
is necessary but not sufficient: the final answer must make economic sense.
\end{keyidea}

Chapters~\ref{chap:consumer-choice} and \ref{chap:cost-choice} apply this
same method to consumer utility and firm profit.

\section*{Chapter Review}

\subsection*{Key concepts}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Function}: a rule linking a dependent variable to one or more inputs.
  \item \textbf{Domain}: the values for which a function is economically and mathematically relevant.
  \item \textbf{Slope}: the change in one variable associated with a one-unit change in another.
  \item \textbf{Derivative}: the instantaneous rate of change of a function.
  \item \textbf{Marginal value}: the economic interpretation of a derivative.
  \item \textbf{Partial derivative}: the effect of changing one variable while holding others fixed.
  \item \textbf{Constraint}: a limit on the feasible choices of an economic agent.
  \item \textbf{Candidate optimum}: a feasible point that may be best but still requires checking.
  \item \textbf{Optimum}: the best feasible choice according to the objective.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Key tools}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Linear equation: $y=a+bx$.
  \item Slope: $\Delta y/\Delta x$.
  \item Power rule: $\frac{d}{dx}x^n=nx^{n-1}$.
  \item At an equilibrium, solve the relevant equations simultaneously and check the result in both.
  \item For a differentiable interior optimum, $f'(x)=0$ identifies a candidate; also check feasible boundary points.
  \item If $f''(x)<0$ at a candidate, it is a strict local maximum.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Exercises}

\subsection*{Conceptual questions}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.]
  \item Distinguish a variable from a parameter. Give one example of each from consumer theory.
  \item For $q(p)=100-2p$, state the input and output, calculate $q(20)$, explain what the parentheses mean, and state an economically meaningful range for $p$.
  \item Explain the economic meaning of the slope of an inverse demand curve. Why does its numerical value differ from the slope of direct demand?
  \item What does a derivative measure? Why are derivatives often called marginal values in economics?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Graphical exercises}
\begin{enumerate}[resume,label=\arabic*.]
  \item Consider the inverse demand curve $p=60-3q$. Find both intercepts, calculate the slope, and draw the curve with clearly labelled axes.
  \item The budget line is $2x+y=10$. Find the intercepts and draw it. How does the line change if income rises and the new constraint is $2x+y=16$?
  \item Explain, using a diagram, the difference between a movement along a demand curve and a rightward shift of demand.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Analytical exercises}
\begin{enumerate}[resume,label=\arabic*.]
  \item Solve: $300-5x=0$; $\frac{120}{x}=6$; and $(x+1)^2=25$.
  \item Calculate the derivative of: (a) $f(x)=x^2$; (b) $f(x)=\frac{3}{4}x^3+7x^2-5x$; and (c) $f(x)=2x^{35}+3x^{27}-5x^{20}+74x-325$. Treat part (c) as a fluency challenge.
  \item Let $f(x,y)=x^2y-xy-6x$. Find $\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}$ and $\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}$.
  \item Let $u(x,y)=x^{1/3}y^{2/3}$. Calculate $MU_x$ and $MU_y$. Do not calculate the MRS; Chapter~4 will interpret their ratio.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Optimisation exercises}
\begin{enumerate}[resume,label=\arabic*.]
  \item A student's net benefit from $h$ revision hours is
  \[
  B(h)=18h-h^2,\qquad 0\leq h\leq12.
  \]
  Calculate marginal net benefit, find the benefit-maximising number of revision hours, verify that it is a maximum, and interpret the result.
  \item A firm has total revenue $R(q)=50q-2q^2$ and total cost $C(q)=10+2q$. Find profit as a function of output and calculate the profit-maximising quantity.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Exam-style multiple-choice questions}
\begin{enumerate}[resume,label=\arabic*.]
  \item If $f(x)=3x^2-4x+7$, then $f'(x)$ is: (a) $6x-4$; (b) $3x-4$; (c) $6x+7$; or (d) $3x^2-4$.
  \item In $p=100-5q$, the number $-5$ is: (a) the vertical intercept; (b) the slope; (c) the quantity demanded when price is zero; or (d) marginal cost.
  \item A partial derivative with respect to $x$ measures: (a) the effect of changing every variable simultaneously; (b) the effect of changing $x$ while holding other variables fixed; (c) the total value of $x$; or (d) the slope of every possible budget line.
  \item In a differentiable optimisation problem, the condition $f'(x)=0$ normally identifies: (a) a guaranteed global maximum; (b) a candidate interior optimum that must still be checked; (c) a point at which the constraint is irrelevant; or (d) a point at which marginal cost must be zero.
\end{enumerate}
