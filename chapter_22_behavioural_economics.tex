\chapter{Behavioural Economics}
\label{chap:behavioural}

\section{Learning objectives}

After completing this chapter, you should be able to explain bounded rationality, heuristics, biases, loss aversion, framing, and the role of behavioural policy.

\section{Beyond the standard model}

\keyterm{Behavioural economics} uses evidence from psychology to refine economic models of decision-making. It does not reject rational-choice analysis entirely. Instead, it investigates systematic departures from its assumptions and asks when they matter for prediction and policy.

People often use \keyterm{heuristics}: mental shortcuts that save time and effort. Heuristics can be useful, but they can also lead to predictable errors. The availability heuristic gives excessive weight to information easily retrieved from memory; anchoring gives excessive influence to an initial number; the representativeness heuristic can lead people to neglect base rates.

\section{Two systems and bias}

Kahneman's distinction between System 1 and System 2 is a useful framework. System 1 is fast, automatic, and intuitive. System 2 is slower, deliberate, and effortful. Neither is always superior: quick judgement is essential in many situations, while careful reasoning can correct some predictable mistakes.

\keyterm{Framing} means that choices can change when the same information is presented differently. \keyterm{Loss aversion} means that losses are often felt more strongly than equivalent gains. These ideas help explain reference dependence, reluctance to sell losing investments, and different responses to gains and losses.

\section{Behavioural public policy}

A \keyterm{nudge} changes the choice environment without removing options or significantly changing monetary incentives. Defaults in pension saving, reminders for medical appointments, and clearer information can influence behaviour. Nudges raise normative questions: who chooses the default, what counts as people's own interests, and when does influence become manipulation?

Behavioural insights complement rather than replace traditional policies. If a harmful activity creates an external cost, a tax may still be appropriate even when information campaigns or defaults are also useful.

\section*{Chapter Review}

\subsection*{Key concepts}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Bounded rationality}: limits on attention, information, and computation.
  \item \textbf{Heuristic}: mental shortcut used in judgement.
  \item \textbf{Anchoring}: undue influence of an initial value.
  \item \textbf{Framing}: choice changes with presentation.
  \item \textbf{Loss aversion}: losses matter more than comparable gains.
  \item \textbf{Nudge}: choice-architecture intervention preserving options.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Exercises}

\subsection*{Conceptual questions}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.]
  \item What is bounded rationality?
  \item Define availability, anchoring, and framing.
  \item Explain loss aversion and give an example.
  \item Distinguish a nudge from a ban or a tax.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Graphical exercises}
\begin{enumerate}[resume,label=\arabic*.]
  \item Draw a reference-dependent value function that is steeper for losses than for gains. Explain each feature.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Analytical exercises}
\begin{enumerate}[resume,label=\arabic*.]
  \item Explain how an opt-out pension default may affect saving while preserving choice.
  \item Give an example in which a behavioural intervention may be less appropriate than a price-based policy.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Exam-style multiple-choice questions}
\begin{enumerate}[resume,label=\arabic*.]
  \item The availability heuristic involves: (a) using all information equally; (b) relying on easily recalled information; (c) ignoring memory; or (d) maximising expected utility exactly.
  \item Loss aversion means that: (a) gains and losses are valued equally; (b) losses are felt more strongly than equivalent gains; (c) people never take risks; or (d) prices cannot affect choice.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{PPE discussion question}

\noindent Are nudges compatible with individual autonomy? Discuss transparency, democratic legitimacy, and the case for preserving freedom of choice.
