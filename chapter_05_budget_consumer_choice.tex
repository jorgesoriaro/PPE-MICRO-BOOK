\chapter{Budget Constraints and Consumer Choice}
\label{chap:consumer-choice}

\section{Learning objectives}

After completing this chapter, you should be able to:
\begin{itemize}
  \item write and graph a consumer's budget set and budget line;
  \item explain the effects of income and price changes on the budget constraint;
  \item identify utility-maximising bundles graphically and algebraically; and
  \item distinguish interior from corner solutions.
\end{itemize}

\section{Preferences meet constraints}

Preferences describe what a consumer would like; a budget constraint describes
what the consumer can afford. One is a wish list, the other is the limit set
by income and prices. Consumer choice combines the two: the consumer selects
a most-preferred bundle among all bundles in the feasible set.

\begin{keyidea}
\textbf{Preferences, prices, and choice.} Indifference curves describe the
trade-offs a consumer \emph{is willing} to make. The budget line describes the
trade-offs the market \emph{requires}. The consumer chooses the most preferred
bundle that is affordable.
\end{keyidea}

Let income be $I$, prices be $p_x$ and $p_y$, and quantities be $x$ and $y$.
Assume throughout that $x,y\geq0$, $p_x,p_y>0$, and $I>0$. The
\keyterm{budget set} is
\[
B=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}_+^2:p_xx+p_yy\leq I\}.
\]
The left-hand side is total expenditure. The inequality allows the consumer
to spend less than income. With monotonic preferences, the consumer normally
chooses a bundle on the \keyterm{budget line}, where all income is spent:
\[
p_xx+p_yy=I.
\]
More precisely, locally non-satiated preferences are enough for budget
exhaustion: if expenditure were below $I$, the consumer could afford a small
increase in a desirable good. If preferences include satiation or a bad, an
optimum need not lie on the budget line. Someone who has already bought all
the tickets they can use, for example, may have no reason to spend the final
euro on another ticket.

\section{The budget line}

Solving the budget line for $y$ gives
\[
y=\frac{I}{p_y}-\frac{p_x}{p_y}x.
\]
The vertical intercept is $I/p_y$: the maximum quantity of $y$ if all income
is spent on $y$. The horizontal intercept is $I/p_x$. The slope is
$-p_x/p_y$, the negative of the relative price of $x$ in terms of $y$. It
shows the market's exchange rate between the two goods.

For example, suppose $I=100$, $p_x=10$, and $p_y=5$. Then
\[
10x+5y=100,
\qquad\text{or}\qquad
y=20-2x.
\]
The intercepts are $(0,20)$ and $(10,0)$, and the slope is $-2$. One extra
unit of $x$ costs two units of $y$: this is the opportunity cost of $x$ in
terms of $y$. If $x$ is a cinema ticket and $y$ is a meal out, the line makes
the trade-off visible.

\section{Changes in income and prices}

When income changes but prices remain fixed, the budget line shifts in a
parallel way. A higher income moves both intercepts outward; a lower income
moves both inward. A scholarship increase, for example, expands the set of
affordable bundles without changing the relative price of the two goods.

When the price of one good changes, the budget line pivots around the
intercept of the good whose price did not change. If $p_x$ falls, $I/p_x$
rises, so the horizontal intercept moves outward while the vertical intercept
stays fixed. The line becomes flatter because $x$ is relatively cheaper. A
discount on rail travel changes the budget line in this way; extra income
does not.

These changes are fundamental for demand analysis. They distinguish a change
in purchasing power from a change in the relative price of goods.

\begin{exercisebox}
\textbf{A student's monthly choices.} Suppose Marta divides a monthly budget
between weekend train journeys and meals out. A rise in her unrestricted
scholarship income shifts her budget line outward in parallel. A youth
railcard lowers the price of train journeys and pivots the budget line instead.
Her preferences may remain unchanged in both cases, yet her chosen bundle can
change.
\end{exercisebox}

\section{Utility maximisation graphically}

With smooth, locally non-satiated, convex preferences, the optimal bundle is
normally the point where the highest attainable indifference curve is tangent
to the budget line. In plain language, there is no small affordable swap that
would make the consumer better off. At a smooth interior optimum with
positive marginal utilities,
\[
MRS_{x,y}=\frac{p_x}{p_y}.
\]
The consumer's willingness to trade $y$ for $x$ equals the trade-off required
by the market. If the MRS were greater than the price ratio, the consumer
would be willing to give up more $y$ for $x$ than the market requires, so
moving toward more $x$ would raise utility. If the MRS were lower, moving
toward more $y$ would raise utility. Tangency is therefore a useful visual
test of whether the two trade-offs agree.

\begin{keyidea}
\textbf{Two trade-offs, one interior choice.}
\[
\underbrace{MRS_{x,y}}_{\text{consumer's willingness to trade}}
=
\underbrace{\frac{p_x}{p_y}}_{\text{market's required trade-off}}.
\]
At a smooth interior optimum, the consumer's personal trade-off equals the
opportunity cost imposed by prices. The equality identifies a candidate
interior solution; corner solutions must still be checked.
\end{keyidea}

When marginal utilities are positive, the same condition can be written as
\[
\frac{MU_x}{p_x}=\frac{MU_y}{p_y}.
\]
At the optimum, the final euro spent on either good produces the same marginal
utility. With strictly convex preferences, a feasible tangency normally
identifies a unique optimum.

\section{Utility maximisation algebraically}

Consider $u(x,y)=xy$, $I=100$, $p_x=10$, and $p_y=5$. We can solve this
problem by reducing it to one choice variable. From the budget constraint,
\[
y=20-2x.
\]
Substitute into utility:
\[
u(x)=x(20-2x)=20x-2x^2.
\]
The first derivative is $20-4x$. Setting it equal to zero gives $x^*=5$.
Substitution into the budget line gives $y^*=10$. The bundle $(5,10)$
exhausts income and maximises utility. Each algebraic step corresponds to the
diagram in the previous section: it locates the best affordable bundle.

Because $y=20-2x$ and both quantities must be non-negative, the feasible
domain is $0\leq x\leq10$. Moreover,
\[
\frac{d^2u}{dx^2}=-4<0,
\]
so utility is strictly concave on this interval. The stationary point $x^*=5$
is therefore the unique maximum; the endpoints give utility zero.

The same answer follows from the tangency condition. Here $MRS_{x,y}=y/x$, while $p_x/p_y=2$. Setting $y/x=2$ gives $y=2x$. Combined with $10x+5y=100$, this yields $x=5$ and $y=10$.

\begin{keyidea}
\textbf{Consumer-choice checklist.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Write the budget set and identify the budget line.
  \item If preferences are locally non-satiated, work on the budget line.
  \item For a smooth interior candidate, set $MRS_{x,y}=p_x/p_y$.
  \item Check feasibility and compare relevant corner bundles.
  \item Interpret the result: what does the price ratio make expensive in terms of the other good?
\end{enumerate}
\end{keyidea}

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{A reusable Cobb--Douglas result.} For
\[
u(x,y)=x^\alpha y^\beta,
\qquad \alpha,\beta>0,
\]
the interior demand bundle is
\[
x^*=\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+\beta}\frac{I}{p_x},
\qquad
y^*=\frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta}\frac{I}{p_y}.
\]
The consumer spends the fraction $\alpha/(\alpha+\beta)$ of income on $x$ and
the fraction $\beta/(\alpha+\beta)$ on $y$.

\section{Interior and corner solutions}

The tangency condition applies only to an \keyterm{interior solution}, where
positive quantities of both goods are consumed. With perfect substitutes, the
best bundle may be a \keyterm{corner solution}: all income is spent on one
good. Think of two nearly identical products where one offers plainly better
value for money.

Suppose $u(x,y)=x+2y$, $p_x=4$, and $p_y=1$. Comparing marginal utility
per euro gives
\[
\frac{MU_x}{p_x}=\frac{1}{4},
\qquad
\frac{MU_y}{p_y}=2.
\]
Every euro spent on $y$ produces more utility than every euro spent on $x$,
so the consumer spends the entire budget on $y$. There is no interior tangency
because the MRS is constant and does not equal the price ratio. It would be
unhelpful to force a tangency calculation where the consumer has no reason to
buy both goods.

For perfect complements, the consumer chooses the kink on the highest attainable indifference curve. The budget line need not be tangent to the indifference curve.

\begin{exercisebox}
\textbf{Worked example: perfect complements.} Let
\[
u(x,y)=\min\{x,y\},
\qquad I=60,
\qquad p_x=2,
\qquad p_y=1.
\]
The consumer wants the goods in a one-to-one proportion, so the optimum must
lie at the kink, where $x=y$. Substitution into the budget line gives
\[
2x+y=60
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
3x=60.
\]
Hence $(x^*,y^*)=(20,20)$. The budget line is not tangent to the L-shaped
indifference curve.
\end{exercisebox}

\section{From consumer choice to demand}

Solving the consumer's problem for different prices produces an individual
demand function. The next chapter develops this link in detail. For now,
remember that demand is not simply a psychological desire for a good. It is
the quantity that maximises utility given prices, income, and preferences.

\section*{Chapter Review}

\subsection*{Key concepts}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Budget set}: all affordable bundles, $p_xx+p_yy\leq I$.
  \item \textbf{Budget line}: bundles that exactly exhaust income.
  \item \textbf{Relative price}: $p_x/p_y$, the opportunity cost of $x$ in units of $y$.
  \item \textbf{Interior solution}: an optimum involving positive amounts of both goods.
  \item \textbf{Corner solution}: an optimum where at least one good is consumed in zero quantity.
  \item \textbf{Tangency condition}: equality between MRS and the relative price at a smooth interior candidate.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Key equations}
\[
p_xx+p_yy=I,
\qquad
y=\frac{I}{p_y}-\frac{p_x}{p_y}x,
\qquad
MRS_{x,y}=\frac{p_x}{p_y},
\qquad
\frac{MU_x}{p_x}=\frac{MU_y}{p_y}.
\]

\section*{Exercises}

\subsection*{Conceptual questions}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.]
  \item Distinguish the budget set from the budget line.
  \item Explain why a consumer with monotonic preferences normally exhausts their budget.
  \item What is the economic interpretation of the slope of a budget line?
  \item Explain the difference between an income change and a change in the price of one good.
  \item Why does the tangency condition not apply to all consumer-choice problems?
\end{enumerate}

  \item Starting from Exercise 6, show graphically what happens when income rises to $180$.
  \item Starting from Exercise 6, show graphically what happens when $p_x$ falls from $12$ to $6$.

\subsection*{Analytical exercises}
\begin{enumerate}[resume,label=\arabic*.]
  \item A consumer has $I=200$, $p_x=20$, $p_y=10$, and $u(x,y)=xy$. Solve for the optimal bundle by substitution and verify it using $MRS_{x,y}=p_x/p_y$.
  \item Solve the consumer problem when $I=100$, $p_x=10$, $p_y=5$, and $u(x,y)=x^{2/3}y^{1/3}$.
  \item Let $u(x,y)=x+3y$, $p_x=2$, $p_y=1$, and $I=40$. Compare marginal utility per euro, find the optimal bundle, and explain why it is a corner solution.
  \item Let $u(x,y)=x+y+xy$, $I=10$, $p_x=2$, and $p_y=1$. Solve the consumer problem and verify the result using the MRS condition.
  \item Let $u(x,y)=x+2y$, $I=40$, $p_x=1$, and $p_y=2$. Show that every bundle on the budget line is optimal. Why does the tangency condition fail to select a unique bundle?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Exam-style multiple-choice questions}
\begin{enumerate}[resume,label=\arabic*.]
  \item The slope of the budget line $p_xx+p_yy=I$ is: (a) $p_x/p_y$; (b) $-p_x/p_y$; (c) $I/p_x$; or (d) $I/p_y$.
  \item If income rises while prices are unchanged, the budget line: (a) pivots around the horizontal intercept; (b) becomes steeper; (c) shifts outward in parallel; or (d) does not change.
  \item At an interior optimum, the consumer chooses a bundle where: (a) income is zero; (b) $MRS_{x,y}=p_x/p_y$; (c) marginal utility is zero for both goods; or (d) both goods have the same price.
\end{enumerate}
