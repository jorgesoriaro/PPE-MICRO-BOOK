After completing this chapter, you should be able to:
\begin{itemize}
  \item distinguish partial from general equilibrium;
  \item use an Edgeworth box to describe feasible exchange allocations;
  \item identify Pareto improvements and the contract curve;
  \item explain competitive equilibrium in an exchange economy; and
  \item state what the welfare theorems do, and do not, establish.
\end{itemize}

\section{From partial to general equilibrium}

Partial-equilibrium analysis zooms in on one market while treating conditions
elsewhere as fixed. This is often a powerful approximation. For example, a
cinema can think about demand for tickets while taking wages, housing costs,
and transport prices as given. The simplification lets us see the main forces
in that one market clearly.

\keyterm{General equilibrium} begins when that simplification is no longer
enough. Markets are connected: a rise in the price of electricity affects
household budgets, firms' costs, demand for appliances, wages in
energy-intensive sectors, and public revenue. General-equilibrium analysis
asks whether all of these decisions can fit together at the same time. In
short, it follows the knock-on effects rather than stopping at the first
market.

\begin{keyidea}
\textbf{An equilibrium is a system, not merely a price.}
In a general equilibrium, consumers choose their preferred affordable bundles,
firms choose profit-maximising plans, and every market clears together. A
change in one market can therefore feed back into the others.
\end{keyidea}

\section{Exchange and the Edgeworth box}

Consider two people, Ana and Bruno, and two goods, $x$ and $y$. The total
amounts are fixed at $\bar{x}$ and $\bar{y}$, so every feasible allocation
satisfies
\[
x_A+x_B=\bar{x},
\qquad
y_A+y_B=\bar{y}.
\]
An \keyterm{Edgeworth box} is a map of all the ways the fixed total can be
shared. Its width is $\bar{x}$ and its height is $\bar{y}$. Ana's origin is
at the lower left; Bruno's is at the upper right. To read Bruno's bundle at
any point, subtract Ana's coordinates from the totals. Thus, every point
describes one complete division of the available goods, not an extra set of
resources.

Figure~\ref{fig:edgeworth-box} uses $\bar{x}=\bar{y}=8$. The initial
allocation $E$ gives Ana $(6,2)$ and Bruno $(2,6)$. The shaded lens contains
allocations that both prefer to $E$ for the illustrated indifference curves.

A \keyterm{Pareto improvement} makes at least one person better off and no
one worse off. An allocation is \keyterm{Pareto efficient} if no Pareto
improvement is feasible. With smooth preferences and positive consumption,
an interior efficient allocation must satisfy
\[
MRS^A_{x,y}=MRS^B_{x,y}.
\]
If the two marginal rates of substitution differed, there would be a small
exchange that both people value: one person would be willing to give up a
little more of one good than the other person requires in return. The
\keyterm{contract curve} is the set of Pareto-efficient allocations in the
box; under the preferences in the figure, it is the diagonal $y_A=x_A$.

\begin{exercisebox}
\textbf{A mutually beneficial exchange.}
Let both people have utility $u(x,y)=xy$. At $E$, Ana has $(6,2)$ and Bruno
has $(2,6)$, so each has utility $12$. At $P$, Ana has $(5,3)$ and Bruno has
$(3,5)$; each has utility $15$. The move from $E$ to $P$ is a Pareto
improvement, not because it makes their bundles equal, but because each
person receives a bundle they prefer.
\end{exercisebox}

\section{Competitive equilibrium in an exchange economy}

At prices $(p_x,p_y)$, each consumer chooses a preferred bundle subject to a
budget constraint. For Ana,
\[
p_xx_A+p_yy_A\leq m_A,
\]
where income $m_A$ can come from her initial endowment and ownership of firms.
A \keyterm{competitive equilibrium} consists of prices and choices such that
each consumer optimises and the feasibility conditions hold.

At a smooth interior competitive equilibrium,
\[
MRS^A_{x,y}=\frac{p_x}{p_y}=MRS^B_{x,y}.
\]
Prices therefore coordinate exchange: each person faces the same relative
price, even though their tastes and initial holdings may differ. A relative
price tells both people the terms on which one good can be traded for another;
it is the common signal that makes their separate choices compatible.

Return to the $8$-by-$8$ economy. If $p_x=p_y=1$ and each person has
purchasing power $8$, a consumer with $u(x,y)=xy$ chooses
\[
(x,y)=(4,4).
\]
The two choices exhaust the total resources, so the allocation clears both
markets. It also lies on the contract curve. This example illustrates how
voluntary exchange can move an economy away from its initial endowment and
toward an efficient allocation.

\section{The welfare theorems}

The \keyterm{First Welfare Theorem} says that, under demanding assumptions,
every competitive equilibrium is Pareto efficient. The assumptions include
price-taking behaviour, well-defined property rights, no externalities,
sufficiently complete markets, and the absence of relevant information
failures. This is a result about a carefully specified model, not an
instruction to leave every real-world market alone. For example, pollution or
a misleading product description breaks precisely the conditions that allow
prices to carry the full social information.

The \keyterm{Second Welfare Theorem} says, under further conditions such as
convex preferences and technologies, that a Pareto-efficient allocation can
be decentralised as a competitive equilibrium after an appropriate
redistribution of initial resources. The basic idea is to distribute initial
resources first, then let prices coordinate exchange. In the theorem,
redistribution is \keyterm{lump-sum}: it changes purchasing power without
changing a marginal incentive. Real taxes and transfers rarely have this ideal
form, which is why the theorem is a guide to thinking rather than a ready-made
policy recipe.

\section{Efficiency is not equity}

Pareto efficiency does not say that an allocation is fair. A society in which
one person owns nearly everything may be Pareto efficient if any transfer from
that person makes them worse off. Efficiency therefore says nothing by itself
about whether the starting distribution was just, or whether it should be
changed. The welfare theorems do not settle questions about equality, rights,
needs, liberty, or political legitimacy.

\begin{keyidea}
\textbf{Keep three questions distinct.}
Economics can ask whether gains from exchange remain, how a policy changes
incentives, and who gains or loses. Deciding whether an outcome is just also
requires ethical and political judgement. Efficiency is important evidence,
not a complete theory of justice.
\end{keyidea}

\section*{Chapter Review}

\subsection*{Key concepts and equations}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Feasibility}: $x_A+x_B=\bar{x}$ and $y_A+y_B=\bar{y}$.
  \item \textbf{Pareto improvement}: someone gains and no one loses.
  \item \textbf{Contract curve}: efficient exchange allocations in an Edgeworth box.
  \item \textbf{Competitive equilibrium}: individual optimisation plus market clearing.
  \item \textbf{Interior exchange efficiency}: $MRS^A_{x,y}=MRS^B_{x,y}$.
  \item \textbf{Welfare theorems}: conditional links between competitive
  equilibrium, efficiency, and redistribution.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Analytical exercises}
\begin{enumerate}[resume,label=\arabic*.]
  \item Total resources are $\bar{x}=\bar{y}=8$, and both Ana and Bruno have $u(x,y)=xy$. Ana initially has $(6,2)$ and Bruno has $(2,6)$. Verify that the allocation $(5,3)$ to Ana is a Pareto improvement. Derive the interior contract curve and find the competitive allocation when $p_x=p_y=1$ and each person's income is $8$.
  \item Total resources are $9$ units of each good. Ana has $u_A=x_A^2y_A$ and Bruno has $u_B=x_By_B^2$. At $p_x=p_y=1$, each has income $9$. Find their demands, check market clearing, and verify the MRS condition.
  \item Why does the Second Welfare Theorem not imply that every real-world redistribution is costless or easy to implement?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Exam-style multiple-choice questions}
\begin{enumerate}[resume,label=\arabic*.]
  \item An allocation is Pareto efficient when: (a) everyone has equal income; (b) no Pareto improvement is feasible; (c) the government does not intervene; or (d) firms earn zero profit.
  \item In an Edgeworth box, a movement from an initial allocation into the mutual-gain lens: (a) makes both people worse off; (b) can make both people better off; (c) must reduce total resources; or (d) proves the allocation is equal.
  \item The Second Welfare Theorem highlights that: (a) equity and efficiency are identical; (b) distribution can be considered before competitive allocation under strong assumptions; (c) all redistributions are costless in reality; or (d) prices are unnecessary.
\end{enumerate}
