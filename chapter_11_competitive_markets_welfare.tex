\section{Learning objectives}

After completing this chapter, you should be able to:
\begin{itemize}
  \item determine competitive market equilibrium;
  \item use comparative statics to analyse shifts in supply and demand;
  \item calculate consumer surplus, producer surplus, and total surplus; and
  \item evaluate the effects of taxes, price ceilings, and price floors.
\end{itemize}

\section{Competitive markets}

A \keyterm{competitive market} has many buyers and sellers, a homogeneous
product, and no individual participant large enough to affect market price.
A market for a standard grade of wheat is closer to this benchmark than a
market for restaurants or smartphones, where firms differ markedly in quality
and branding. Buyers and sellers are price takers. Competition is an ideal
type: real markets may approximate it to different degrees.

Market demand gives the quantity consumers wish to buy at each price; market
supply gives the quantity firms wish to sell at each price. A
\keyterm{competitive equilibrium}, or \keyterm{market-clearing} outcome, is a
price and quantity at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied:
\[
Q_D(p^*)=Q_S(p^*).
\]

\begin{keyidea}
\textbf{A three-step equilibrium method.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Write demand and supply using the same price variable.
  \item Set $Q_D(p)=Q_S(p)$ and solve for the equilibrium price $p^*$.
  \item Substitute $p^*$ into either curve to find $Q^*$, then check that the
  two quantities agree.
\end{enumerate}
The calculation identifies a market-clearing point. The economic model then
explains why excess demand tends to raise price and excess supply tends to
lower it.
\end{keyidea}

At prices above equilibrium, there is \keyterm{excess supply}, placing
downward pressure on price. At prices below equilibrium, there is
\keyterm{excess demand}, placing upward pressure on price. A shop with
unsold seasonal stock may reduce its price; a fully booked service may raise
its price or ask customers to wait. This adjustment story describes a
tendency, not necessarily an instantaneous process.

\section{Comparative statics}

\keyterm{Comparative statics} compares equilibria before and after a change in
an underlying condition. It is the language economists use to ask what changes
when one part of a market moves. An increase in demand shifts the demand curve
rightward, raising equilibrium price and quantity when supply slopes upward.
An increase in production cost shifts supply leftward, raising price and
reducing quantity. For example, poor weather that reduces a harvest can shift
the supply of a crop left even when consumers' tastes have not changed.

Comparative statics is often more valuable than a single equilibrium
calculation. It identifies which changes are expected to affect prices,
quantities, and distribution, while leaving open the empirical question of
their magnitude.

\section{Consumer surplus, producer surplus, and total surplus}

\keyterm{Consumer surplus} is the difference between consumers' willingness to
pay and the amount they actually pay. If you would have paid \euro8 for a
book but buy it for \euro6, your consumer surplus from that purchase is
\euro2. In a demand diagram, it is the area below demand and above market
price for units traded. \keyterm{Producer surplus} is the difference between
the price received and the minimum amount required for firms to supply each
unit; it is the area above supply and below market price.

For a price-taking firm in the short run, producer surplus equals revenue minus
variable cost. It is not the same as economic profit: fixed cost still has to
be paid, so
\[
\pi=PS-FC
\]
when the firm's supply curve reflects marginal cost. This distinction links
the welfare diagram to the shutdown analysis in Chapter~\ref{chap:cost-curves}.

For linear curves, these areas are often triangles. If demand intercepts the
price axis at $P_{\max}$, equilibrium price is $p^*$, and equilibrium quantity
is $Q^*$, consumer surplus is
\[
CS=\frac{1}{2}(P_{\max}-p^*)Q^*.
\]

\section{Efficiency and total surplus}

In an untaxed competitive market without external effects,
\keyterm{total surplus} is consumer surplus plus producer surplus. When a tax
is present, standard total surplus also includes tax revenue; when external
effects are relevant, it also accounts for external benefits or costs. Under
the untaxed, no-externality conditions, competitive equilibrium maximises
total surplus: units for which willingness to pay exceeds marginal cost are
traded, while units for which marginal cost exceeds willingness to pay are
not. The logic is simple: a trade is counted as a gain when the buyer values
the unit more than the resources needed to make it.

This conclusion is conditional. It requires that prices reflect relevant costs
and benefits, that market participants have suitable information, and that the
distribution of purchasing power is not itself the criterion for justice.
Efficiency asks whether potential gains from trade are exhausted; equity asks
how resources and opportunities are distributed. Later chapters study cases in
which the conditions for this welfare result fail.

\section{Taxes}

Relative to an untaxed competitive market with no external effects, a per-unit
tax creates a \keyterm{tax wedge} between the price paid by buyers and the
price received by sellers:
\[
p_B=p_S+t.
\]
Tax revenue is collected by government. When the tax reduces the quantity
traded, part of the potential surplus from mutually beneficial trade disappears
relative to that benchmark. This loss is \keyterm{deadweight loss}. Tax revenue
is a transfer from private parties to government; it is the forgone trades,
rather than the transfer itself, that create the deadweight loss. In a market
with an external effect, however, a corrective tax can move quantity toward the
efficient level and increase total surplus.

The legal incidence of a tax does not determine its economic incidence.
Whether the tax is formally paid by buyers or sellers, its burden is shared
according to relative elasticities. The less elastic side of the market bears
more of the burden because it has fewer alternatives. A tax collected from
petrol stations, for instance, can still be reflected partly in the price paid
by drivers.

\begin{exercisebox}
\textbf{A worked tax calculation.}
Suppose
\[
Q_D=120-2p_B,\qquad Q_S=2p_S,
\]
and a tax of \euro6 per unit is introduced. Substitution of
$p_B=p_S+6$ gives
\[
120-2(p_S+6)=2p_S
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
p_S=27,\qquad p_B=33,\qquad Q_t=54.
\]
Tax revenue is $6(54)=\euro324$. Before the tax, price is \euro30 and quantity
is $60$, so the tax reduces mutually beneficial trade by six units.
\end{exercisebox}

\section{Price controls}

A \keyterm{price ceiling} is a legal maximum price. If it is set below
equilibrium, quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied, creating a shortage.
Allocation may then occur through waiting, queues, informal payments,
rationing, or discrimination rather than price. In that case, a lower posted
price does not mean that every prospective buyer receives the good: some may
instead pay through time spent searching or waiting.

A \keyterm{price floor} is a legal minimum price. If it is set above
equilibrium, quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded, creating a surplus.
Policy design must then address what happens to unsold output or unemployed
resources.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{p{0.21\textwidth}p{0.24\textwidth}p{0.42\textwidth}}
\toprule
Policy & Binding when & Main consequence \\
\midrule
Price ceiling & Set below $p^*$ & Shortage: $Q_D>Q_S$; allocation can shift to queues, quality changes, or rationing. \\
Price floor & Set above $p^*$ & Surplus: $Q_S>Q_D$; policy must address unsold output or excess supply of labour. \\
Non-binding control & Ceiling at or above $p^*$, or floor at or below $p^*$ & No immediate effect on the competitive equilibrium. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{exercisebox}
\textbf{A capped rental market.}
A rent ceiling may aim to protect tenants, yet a binding ceiling also changes
who receives the available homes and how landlords maintain or allocate them.
The relevant evaluation is therefore wider than the posted rent: it includes
shortages, waiting time, housing quality, and distributional goals. Economics
clarifies these trade-offs; it does not decide the policy objective by itself.
\end{exercisebox}

Price controls can pursue equity or stability objectives, but their effects
cannot be assessed only by observing the controlled price. The quantity
available, method of allocation, quality, and incentives to enter or exit the
market also matter. This is why the same policy can look attractive to a
tenant who obtains a home and frustrating to someone who cannot find one.

\section*{Chapter Review}

\subsection*{Key concepts and equations}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Market equilibrium}: $Q_D=Q_S$.
  \item \textbf{Consumer surplus}: willingness to pay minus expenditure.
  \item \textbf{Producer surplus}: price received minus minimum supply cost;
  in the short run, $\pi=PS-FC$ for a price-taking firm.
  \item \textbf{Total surplus}: $CS+PS$ in an untaxed market without
  external effects; add tax revenue and external effects when relevant.
  \item \textbf{Tax wedge}: $p_B-p_S=t$.
  \item \textbf{Deadweight loss}: surplus lost because beneficial trades do not occur.
  \item \textbf{Tax incidence}: the actual division of a tax burden.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Exercises}

\subsection*{Conceptual questions}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.]
  \item Define competitive market equilibrium. What creates pressure for price adjustment away from equilibrium?
  \item Distinguish consumer surplus, producer surplus, and economic profit.
  \item Why does a competitive equilibrium maximise total surplus only under particular assumptions?
  \item Explain why legal tax incidence need not equal economic tax incidence.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Graphical exercises}
\begin{enumerate}[resume,label=\arabic*.]
  \item Draw supply and demand. Mark equilibrium, consumer surplus, producer surplus, and total surplus.
  \item Show the effect of a per-unit tax. Mark buyer and seller prices, tax revenue, and deadweight loss.
  \item Draw a binding price ceiling and identify the shortage. Explain why a ceiling at or above equilibrium is non-binding.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Analytical exercises}
\begin{enumerate}[resume,label=\arabic*.]
  \item Let $Q_D=120-2P$ and $Q_S=2P$. Find the competitive equilibrium.
  \item Using Exercise 8, calculate consumer surplus, producer surplus, and total surplus.
  \item A tax of \euro6 per unit is imposed in the market of Exercise 8. Find the new quantity, buyer price, seller price, tax revenue, and deadweight loss.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Exam-style multiple-choice questions}
\begin{enumerate}[resume,label=\arabic*.]
  \item A binding price ceiling is set: (a) above equilibrium price; (b) below equilibrium price; (c) exactly at every possible price; or (d) only in monopolies.
  \item A tax generally creates deadweight loss because it: (a) makes all trade impossible; (b) reduces the quantity of mutually beneficial trades; (c) eliminates tax revenue; or (d) raises consumer surplus.
  \item The less elastic side of a market generally bears: (a) less of a tax; (b) more of a tax; (c) no tax; or (d) all producer surplus.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{PPE discussion question}

\noindent Competitive equilibrium can be efficient while leaving some people
with very little purchasing power. Discuss why efficiency and justice are
distinct criteria for evaluating market outcomes.
