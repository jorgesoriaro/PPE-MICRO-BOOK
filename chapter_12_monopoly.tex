\section{Learning objectives}

After completing this chapter, you should be able to:
\begin{itemize}
  \item identify the sources of monopoly power;
  \item derive a monopolist's output and price choice;
  \item compare monopoly with perfect competition; and
  \item evaluate the welfare and policy implications of monopoly.
\end{itemize}

\section{What is monopoly?}

A \keyterm{monopoly} is a market with one seller and substantial
\keyterm{barriers to entry}. A monopolist has \keyterm{market power}: it
recognises that selling more output requires a lower price. Monopoly is not
defined merely by a firm's large size; it depends on whether alternatives and
potential entry constrain the firm.

Sources of market power include legal protection such as \keyterm{patents},
control of an essential input, network effects, economies of scale, and
regulatory barriers. Some sources may reward innovation or reduce costs; others
may arise from exclusionary practices or political privilege. Their policy
implications differ.

In a competitive market, an individual firm takes price as given and produces
where $p=MC$. A monopolist faces the market demand curve: selling more requires
a lower price. That is why its marginal revenue is below price and why its
choice rule changes to $MR=MC$.

\section{Demand, revenue, and marginal revenue}

For a \keyterm{uniform-price monopolist} facing downward-sloping inverse
demand, marginal revenue is below price. To sell one additional unit, the firm
lowers price not only on that unit but also on units it was already selling.
If inverse demand is $p(Q)$, total revenue is
\[
TR(Q)=p(Q)Q.
\]
Differentiating gives
\[
MR(Q)=\frac{dTR}{dQ}
=p(Q)+Q\frac{dp(Q)}{dQ}.
\]
When demand slopes downward, $dp/dQ<0$, so $MR(Q)<p(Q)$ for positive output.

\begin{exercisebox}
\textbf{Why the next unit brings in less than its price.}
If $p=20-Q$, selling $5$ units gives price $15$ and revenue $75$. Selling
$6$ units requires price $14$ and gives revenue $84$. The sixth unit brings
in marginal revenue of $9$, not $14$, because the lower price also applies to
the first five units.
\end{exercisebox}

For linear inverse demand $p=a-bQ$,
\[
TR(Q)=aQ-bQ^2
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
MR(Q)=a-2bQ.
\]
The marginal-revenue curve has the same vertical intercept as demand and twice
its slope.

\section{Profit maximisation}

The monopolist chooses output to maximise
\[
\pi(Q)=TR(Q)-C(Q).
\]
For a differentiable interior candidate,
\[
\frac{d\pi}{dQ}=MR(Q)-MC(Q)=0,
\qquad\text{so}\qquad
MR(Q)=MC(Q).
\]
The firm must still check feasibility and confirm that marginal profit falls
through zero. With a concave profit function, $\pi''(Q)<0$ confirms a local
maximum. After choosing output, the firm reads price from the demand curve.

\begin{keyidea}
\textbf{The monopoly sequence.}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Start with inverse demand, $p(Q)$.
  \item Calculate $TR(Q)=p(Q)Q$ and then $MR(Q)$.
  \item Set $MR(Q)=MC(Q)$ to find monopoly output.
  \item Return to demand to find monopoly price.
  \item Calculate profit and compare output with the competitive benchmark.
\end{enumerate}
\end{keyidea}

Suppose
\[
p=50-2Q
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
C(Q)=10+2Q.
\]
Then $MR=50-4Q$ and $MC=2$. Setting $MR=MC$ gives
\[
50-4Q=2
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
Q_M=12.
\]
Demand then gives $p_M=26$. Profit is revenue minus cost:
\[
\pi=26(12)-[10+2(12)]=278.
\]

A monopoly does not have a supply curve analogous to that of a competitive
firm: its chosen quantity depends jointly on demand and cost, not on price
alone.

\section{Monopoly versus competition}

With the same demand and marginal cost, a competitive market produces where
$p=MC$. In the running example, competition yields
\[
Q_C=24
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
p_C=2.
\]

shows the sequence $MR=MC\rightarrow Q_M\rightarrow p_M$ and the welfare

Some consumers who value the good above marginal cost do not buy it, creating
a \keyterm{deadweight loss}. Monopoly also redistributes surplus. Consumers
who continue to buy pay more, transferring part of their surplus to the firm.
This \keyterm{transfer of surplus} is not itself a deadweight loss; the loss
arises from units that are not traded even though their value to consumers
exceeds their production cost.

With linear demand and constant marginal cost, the deadweight-loss triangle is
\[
DWL=\frac{1}{2}(Q_C-Q_M)\bigl[p(Q_M)-MC\bigr].
\]
For the running example, this is
\[
DWL=\frac{1}{2}(24-12)(26-2)=144.
\]

More generally, if $p=a-bQ$, marginal cost is constant at $c$, and $a>c$, then
\[
Q_M=\frac{a-c}{2b},
\qquad
p_M=\frac{a+c}{2},
\]
whereas the competitive benchmark is
\[
Q_C=\frac{a-c}{b},
\qquad
p_C=c.
\]
Thus $Q_M<Q_C$ and $p_M>p_C$ under these standard assumptions.

\section{Price discrimination}

\keyterm{Price discrimination} occurs when a seller charges different prices
to buyers for reasons other than differences in cost. It requires market power,
the ability to distinguish buyers or their willingness to pay, and limited
resale between buyers. Different prices caused solely by different costs of
serving buyers are not price discrimination in the economic sense.

Under perfect \keyterm{first-degree price discrimination}, the seller charges
each buyer their maximum willingness to pay. Output can reach the efficient
level, but the seller captures almost all surplus. More common forms include
quantity discounts, student prices, peak pricing, and different fares for
business and leisure travellers; these may reflect differences in willingness
to pay or demand elasticity.

\section{Policy toward monopoly}

Possible responses include \keyterm{competition law}, price regulation, public
ownership, procurement, and patent policy. No response is universally best. A
\keyterm{natural monopoly}, where one firm can serve the market at lower cost
than several firms, may require regulation rather than forced fragmentation.

\begin{exercisebox}
\textbf{One network or many?}
A town may be able to support one water-pipe network more cheaply than several
duplicated networks. This is a natural-monopoly problem. It does not by itself
tell us whether the provider should be private, public, regulated, or
competitive at another stage; those are institutional and policy questions.
\end{exercisebox}

Innovation incentives, administrative capacity, and distributional goals must
be considered alongside static efficiency. A high price alone is not proof of
monopoly power: it may also reflect high cost, temporary scarcity, quality, or
taxation. The relevant question is whether a seller can persistently raise price
above competitive levels because entry and alternatives do not constrain it.

\section*{Chapter Review}

\subsection*{Key concepts and equations}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Monopoly}: one seller protected by barriers to entry.
  \item \textbf{Uniform-price monopoly rule}: $MR=MC$, then read price from demand.
  \item \textbf{Marginal revenue}: $MR=p(Q)+Q\,dp/dQ$, which is below price for downward-sloping demand.
  \item \textbf{No competitive-style supply curve}: monopoly output depends on both demand and cost.
  \item \textbf{Deadweight loss}: surplus from mutually beneficial units between $Q_M$ and $Q_C$ that are not traded.
  \item \textbf{Price discrimination}: different prices not explained by cost; it can change both output and distribution.
\end{itemize}

  \item A profit-maximising monopolist chooses output where: (a) $p=MC$; (b) $MR=MC$; (c) $TR=TC$; or (d) $MR=p$.
  \item Relative to perfect competition, monopoly normally produces: (a) more output at a lower price; (b) less output at a higher price; (c) the same output; or (d) zero output.
  \item First-degree price discrimination can: (a) eliminate all trade; (b) increase output toward the efficient level; (c) make marginal revenue exceed price; or (d) remove market power.
\end{enumerate}
