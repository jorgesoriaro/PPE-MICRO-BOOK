\chapter{Market Failures}

\section{Learning objectives}

After completing this chapter, you should be able to:
\begin{itemize}
  \item diagnose why a private market outcome can diverge from a social objective;
  \item analyse negative and positive externalities using social marginal costs and benefits;
  \item distinguish public goods, common resources, club goods, and private goods;
  \item explain adverse selection, moral hazard, signalling, and screening; and
  \item compare realistic responses to a market failure.
\end{itemize}

\section{When markets do not deliver efficient outcomes}

Competitive markets can coordinate decentralised decisions effectively, but
their efficiency result depends on demanding assumptions. A \keyterm{market
failure} arises when private choices do not account properly for all relevant
costs, benefits, information, or collective-action problems. The phrase does
not mean that every market is harmful or that public action is automatically
better. It tells us where the private decision and the wider social objective
come apart.

\begin{keyidea}
\textbf{Three routes from private choice to a social problem.}
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{3.1cm}p{4.6cm}p{4.2cm}@{}}
\toprule
Problem & What is missing from the private decision? & Typical result \\
\midrule
Externality & Effects on third parties & Too much or too little activity \\
Public good or common resource & Exclusion, ownership, or coordination & Under-provision or overuse \\
Information asymmetry & Relevant information about type or action & Bad trades, missing trade, or excess risk \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\end{keyidea}

This chapter develops these three topics in turn. The diagnosis alone is not a
policy conclusion: a proposed remedy must also be compared with feasible
alternatives, as Chapter~15 emphasised.

\section{Externalities}

An \keyterm{externality} occurs when an action affects people not directly
involved in a market transaction and the effect is not reflected in the market
price. A factory's emissions may harm neighbours; vaccination may reduce
infection risk for others. These are, respectively, negative and positive
externalities. The buyer and seller make a private deal, but people outside
that deal also feel part of its consequences.

With a negative externality,
\[
MSC=MPC+MEC,
\]
where $MPC$ is marginal private cost and $MEC$ is \keyterm{marginal external
cost}. With a positive externality,
\[
MSB=MPB+MEB,
\]
where $MPB$ is marginal private benefit and $MEB$ is marginal external benefit.
Efficiency requires
\[
MSB(Q^*)=MSC(Q^*).
\]
For a negative externality, firms and consumers use private incentives, so the
market quantity is typically too high: $Q_M>Q^*$. For a positive externality,
private demand can understate social benefit, so $Q_M<Q^*$. For example, a
driver deciding whether to make one more congested journey normally considers
their own time and fuel, not the small delay imposed on every other road user.

A \keyterm{Pigouvian tax} sets
\[
t^*=MEC(Q^*).
\]
It makes the private marginal decision reflect the social marginal cost. In
Figure~\ref{fig:negative-externality}, the tax is a wedge between what buyers
pay and sellers receive. The aim is not to punish every producer, but to make
the price signal include the harm caused by one more unit. Tax revenue is a
transfer, not the deadweight loss; the loss is the surplus from harmful extra
units between $Q^*$ and $Q_M$.

Other responses include subsidies for external benefits, direct regulation,
and \keyterm{tradable permits}. The \keyterm{Coase theorem} provides a useful
benchmark: with clearly defined transferable rights and negligible transaction
costs, bargaining can reach an efficient quantity. Two neighbours deciding
how late one may play loud music are a small-scale illustration: an agreement
may be possible when there are only two parties and the effect is easy to
observe. The initial assignment of rights affects who is compensated, even if
it does not affect the efficient quantity under these strong assumptions. In
practice, many affected parties, information problems, credit constraints, and
enforcement costs often prevent such bargaining.

\section{Public goods and common resources}

A good exhibits \keyterm{rivalry} if one person's consumption reduces what is
available to others. It is \keyterm{excludable} if non-payers can be prevented
from consuming it. These two properties create a useful classification.

  \centering
  \caption{Rivalry and excludability}
  \label{tab:goods-classification}
  \begingroup
  \small
  \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.18}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}p{2.1cm}p{4.7cm}p{4.7cm}@{}}
    \toprule
     & Excludable & Non-excludable \\
    \midrule
    Rival &
    Private good: bread, clothing &
    Common resource: an open fishery \\
    Non-rival &
    Club good: a subscription platform before congestion &
    Public good: street lighting or national defence \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \endgroup
\end{table}
\FloatBarrier

A \keyterm{public good} is non-rival and non-excludable. Non-excludability
encourages \keyterm{free riding}: people may enjoy a benefit without
contributing, so private provision can be too low. A well-lit street is useful
to a resident who pays and to a passer-by who does not; that is precisely what
makes voluntary collection difficult. For a public good, individual marginal
benefits are added \emph{vertically}:
\[
MSB(Q)=MB_A(Q)+MB_B(Q)+\cdots.
\]
Public provision does not itself make a good public; the classification depends
on rivalry and excludability.

\begin{exercisebox}
\textbf{A flood barrier and free riding.}
Two neighbouring households each value a flood barrier at \euro8, while the
barrier costs \euro12 in total. Provision is socially worthwhile because total
benefit is \euro16. Yet neither household may wish to pay the full cost alone,
hoping that the other will provide it. This is the free-rider problem.
\end{exercisebox}

A \keyterm{common resource} is rival but non-excludable. An open fishery can
therefore be overused: each fisher considers their private catch but not the
effect of depletion on others. This is the \keyterm{tragedy of the commons}.
Possible responses include quotas, property or use rights, user fees, and
community governance. The tragedy is not inevitable: local rules, monitoring,
social norms, and collective ownership can sometimes sustain shared resources.

\section{Information asymmetry}

\keyterm{Information asymmetry} exists when one party to a transaction knows
something relevant that the other does not. Timing matters. Before a contract,
hidden information about a person's type or quality can create
\keyterm{adverse selection}. After a contract, hidden action can create
\keyterm{moral hazard}. Insurance offers a useful timeline: before buying a
policy, a driver may know more than the insurer about their risk; after buying
it, the insurer may not fully observe how carefully the driver behaves.

A \keyterm{signal} is an action by the informed party that can credibly convey
information. A warranty can signal quality only when it is sufficiently more
costly for low-quality sellers to imitate than for high-quality sellers.
\keyterm{Screening} is an action by the less-informed party that induces
self-selection, such as an insurer offering contracts with different
deductibles. The distinction is simple: a signal is sent by the person who
knows more; screening is designed by the person who knows less.

\begin{exercisebox}
\textbf{Why good products can disappear.}
Suppose half of used cars are high quality, worth \euro10 to buyers and
requiring at least \euro8 for sellers to offer them. The other half are low
quality, worth \euro4 to buyers and requiring at least \euro2 for sellers. If
buyers expect an equal mixture, they pay at most \euro7. High-quality sellers
then leave because \euro7 is below \euro8. Buyers consequently expect only
low-quality cars and pay at most \euro4. Profitable trade in high-quality cars
disappears because quality was private information before the contract.
\end{exercisebox}

Reputation, disclosure rules, certification, consumer protection, and contract
design can improve information and trade. They can also impose costs, exclude
some participants, or be manipulated, so their design matters.

\section{A common lesson}

A market failure does not automatically establish that any government
intervention will improve matters. It identifies a divergence between private
incentives and a social objective. The next question is comparative
institutional analysis: which realistic rule, tax, service, regulation, or
private arrangement is most likely to improve the outcome, given information
limits, enforcement capacity, and political incentives? The useful habit is
to move from diagnosis to a comparison of workable responses, rather than
from diagnosis straight to a preferred slogan.

\section*{Chapter Review}

\subsection*{Key concepts and equations}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Negative externality}: $MSC=MPC+MEC$ and typically $Q_M>Q^*$.
  \item \textbf{Positive externality}: $MSB=MPB+MEB$ and typically $Q_M<Q^*$.
  \item \textbf{Corrective tax}: $t^*=MEC(Q^*)$.
  \item \textbf{Public good}: non-rival and non-excludable; benefits add vertically.
  \item \textbf{Common resource}: rival and non-excludable; vulnerable to overuse.
  \item \textbf{Information asymmetry}: hidden type before, or hidden action after, a contract.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Exercises}

\subsection*{Conceptual questions}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.]
  \item Define a market failure. Why does its existence not automatically imply that intervention will improve outcomes?
  \item Distinguish a negative externality from a positive externality using social marginal cost and benefit.
  \item Explain why a public good and a common resource both create collective-action problems but tend to fail in opposite directions.
  \item Distinguish adverse selection from moral hazard using insurance examples.
  \item When is a warranty a credible signal of quality?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Graphical exercises}
\begin{enumerate}[resume,label=\arabic*.]
  \item Draw demand, marginal private cost, and marginal social cost for a negative production externality. Mark market output, efficient output, the corrective tax, and deadweight loss.
  \item Two residents have different marginal benefits from a flood barrier. Draw their marginal-benefit curves and show why they must be added vertically for a public good.
  \item Draw a used-car market and explain how uncertainty about quality can reduce trade.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Analytical exercises}
\begin{enumerate}[resume,label=\arabic*.]
  \item Demand is $P=100-Q$, private marginal cost is $20$, and marginal external cost is $20$. Find market output, efficient output, the corrective tax, tax revenue after correction, and the deadweight loss before correction.
  \item Explain how transferable fishing quotas can reduce overfishing. What distributional issue can arise when quotas are initially allocated?
  \item Two residents have marginal benefits
  \[
  MB_A=20-Q,
  \qquad
  MB_B=16-Q,
  \]
  from a non-rival public good. Marginal cost is $MC=12$. Find the efficient quantity. Should individual marginal-benefit curves be added vertically or horizontally?
  \item Explain how a warranty can reduce adverse selection in a used-goods market. State the condition needed for it to be informative.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Exam-style multiple-choice questions}
\begin{enumerate}[resume,label=\arabic*.]
  \item A negative externality exists when: (a) private cost exceeds social cost; (b) social cost exceeds private cost; (c) all firms make losses; or (d) price is zero.
  \item A public good is: (a) rival and excludable; (b) non-rival and non-excludable; (c) rival and non-excludable; or (d) supplied by government by definition.
  \item Hidden information before a contract is associated with: (a) moral hazard; (b) adverse selection; (c) perfect competition; or (d) price-taking.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent Choose one policy problem such as climate change, health insurance,
housing, or fisheries. Identify the market failure and compare two realistic
institutional responses in terms of efficiency, equity, and political
feasibility.
